01RolloutJ5R8N43

Three Use Cases for AI Rollout

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. If you only had budget for one AI initiative this year, would you lean toward internal efficiency or external differentiation — and why?
01

Adopt AI products internally

ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, GitHub Copilot/Cursor, Adobe Firefly.

  • Fastest time-to-value
  • Low investment
  • Generic, not specialized
02

Build proprietary AI for employees

Meeting summarizers, IT support copilots, knowledge management systems.

  • Tailored to your business
  • Data stays in-house (if built right)
  • Requires investment & expertise
03

Build proprietary AI for customers

AI-powered products/services, customer-facing chatbots, differentiated offerings.

  • Competitive advantage
  • Revenue potential
  • Highest investment & risk
What we just learned

The three use cases sit on a single risk/investment gradient: adopting existing tools is fast and cheap but generic, internal proprietary AI trades investment for a tailored efficiency gain, and customer-facing proprietary AI carries the highest cost and risk but also the only path to genuine competitive differentiation. Most organizations should move through them roughly in order, not start at the top.

🎯 CTO view

Use Case 1 isn't just a warm-up — it's how the organization builds the AI literacy and workflow muscle that Use Cases 2 and 3 actually depend on. Skipping straight to a customer-facing AI product without that foundation raises execution risk substantially.

02CultureT2M9K76

Leading by Example

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. Why would a leader being visibly proud of using AI actually change adoption rates across a whole team?
"You should be proud of using AI, not shy about it."
01

Use AI tools openly

Draft documents, contracts, RFPs with ChatGPT. Use Claude/Gemini for research. Be transparent, show teams how you use it.

02

Be an early adopter

Microsoft Copilot, AI meeting note generators. Share efficiency gains. Make AI usage a positive example.

03

Encourage your teams

Tech → GitHub Copilot/Cursor. Creative → Adobe Firefly. Sales → Salesforce Einstein. Everyone → ChatGPT/Claude.

04

Recognize early adopters

Call out AI wins in meetings. Make positive examples of early adopters. Reward experimentation. Create AI champions.

What we just learned

Visible leadership usage removes the social risk that otherwise suppresses adoption — employees who see AI use treated as shameful or a shortcut hide it, while employees who see it modeled and rewarded openly experiment and share what works, which is exactly the behavior that made Morgan Stanley's 98% adoption number (Topic 4) possible.

📋 PM view

"Recognize early adopters" needs an actual mechanism (a channel, a meeting agenda item, a small reward) — good intentions without a repeatable ritual tend to fade within a quarter.

  1. RISEN is described as an inference-time optimization. What does that tell you about who can use it — does it require any technical setup at all?
FIG 3.1 — RISEN: FIVE PARTS OF A GOOD PROMPTinference-time optimization — no code required

What we just learned

RISEN is entirely an inference-time technique — it changes nothing about the model itself, only how you talk to it, which means anyone with prompt access (no engineering setup, no budget approval) can use it immediately. That's exactly what makes it the cheapest, fastest lever in the whole optimization toolkit, and why it's worth mastering before reaching for RAG or fine-tuning.

📋 PM view

RISEN is the right first thing to teach every employee adopting AI tools (Topic 2) — it's zero-cost, immediately actionable, and often closes most of the gap between a mediocre and a genuinely useful output.

05OptimizationB4Q7L18

Training-Time vs Inference-Time Optimizations

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. If inference-time optimizations are faster and lower-risk, why would anyone still invest in training-time optimizations like fine-tuning?
FIG 5.1 — TWO OPTIMIZATION SURFACESimproving the model vs improving how you use it
What we just learned

Training-time optimization changes the model's weights permanently; inference-time optimization leaves weights frozen and works around the model instead. Fine-tuning remains worth the investment specifically when you need the model to internalize a new skill or generalize to novel cases the way BloombergGPT does for finance — inference-time tricks alone can't teach a model a genuinely new capability, only guide how it applies what it already knows.

🏗️ Architect view

Default to exhausting inference-time options (RISEN, then RAG) before considering fine-tuning — it's the lower-cost, lower-risk path, and many "we need to fine-tune" requests turn out to be solvable with better prompting or retrieval alone.

06RAGK9F2D65

RAG — Deep Dive

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. If RAG is "just adding context to the prompt," why is it treated as such a big deal in the industry?
  2. Encoding LLMs turn text into vectors where similar meaning = similar numbers. What does that imply about how these models represent "meaning"?

The small idea: add background information to prompts. The big idea: use encoding LLMs to find that background information via semantic search — turning text into vectors (numbers) that represent meaning, not just matching words.

FIG 6.1 — RAG WORKFLOWquestion → vector → search → retrieve → answer
Without RAG

User: "I can't access Gmail."
LLM: "Check your network connection..." (generic)

With RAG

Context: "Company policy: no personal email. Access denied = firewall."
LLM: "You're blocked by the company firewall due to policy. Please request approval..."

What we just learned

RAG really is "just" adding relevant context to a prompt — but the "big idea" that makes it powerful at scale is semantic search: encoding text into vectors lets you retrieve relevant information by meaning rather than exact keyword match, which is what makes it practical to ground answers in thousands of documents instead of one hand-picked paragraph. Whether meaning is "just math" is genuinely debated — what's not in dispute is that the vector representation captures enough of it to make retrieval reliably useful in practice.

🎯 CTO view

RAG is usually the highest-leverage first move for reducing hallucinations in a business context — it's inference-time (low risk, fast), and it directly targets the failure mode (the model not knowing your specific facts) that causes most embarrassing wrong answers.

07Fine TuningR1T6M84

Fine Tuning — High Risk, High Reward

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. What's the difference between a model "knowing a fact" and a model "having learned a skill" — and why does that distinction matter for choosing fine-tuning?

Definition: taking a pre-trained model and training it further with your proprietary data (prompts + responses), so it becomes specialized for your domain.

⚠️ High risk
  • Significant R&D required
  • Outcomes are unclear — might not work
  • Expensive (GPUs, time, expertise)
  • "Catastrophic forgetting" risk if over-trained
✅ High reward
  • Can outperform GPT on your specific task
  • Real differentiation, competitive advantage
  • Faster inference (no RAG lookup needed)
  • Learns general skills, not just facts

Example: e-commerce pricing

Without fine-tuningWith fine-tuning
Generic pricing advice, doesn't understand your categories, can't estimate value from descriptionsLearns your product catalog, estimates prices from descriptions, can value new products it's never seen — outperforms GPT on this specific task
What we just learned

A model "knowing a fact" means it can recall or look up a specific piece of information (which RAG can supply on demand); a model "having learned a skill" means it can generalize a pattern to new, unseen cases — like estimating a price for a product it's never encountered, based on the pattern learned across thousands of examples. That generalization is precisely what RAG can't provide (retrieval only returns what's already written down), which is why fine-tuning is the right tool when the goal is a transferable capability, not just accessible facts.

🏗️ Architect view

Catastrophic forgetting is a real, measurable risk — validate the fine-tuned model against its original general capabilities, not just the new specialized task, before deploying it. A model that's brilliant at pricing but has lost basic language competence is a net loss.

RAG vs Fine Tuning

  1. If you had unlimited budget but limited data, which would you lean toward? What if the reverse — unlimited data but limited budget?

Optimization typeInference-timeTraining-time

💡 Rule of thumb
Fixed information → RAG. New skills → Fine Tuning. And you can do both — a fine-tuned model plus RAG on top is the best of both worlds.
What we just learned

RAG and fine-tuning solve genuinely different problems and the comparison table makes the decision almost mechanical: if your gap is "the model doesn't know our current facts," that's RAG; if your gap is "the model can't perform our specific kind of task well," that's fine-tuning. Unlimited budget with limited data points toward RAG (data quality/volume constrains fine-tuning more directly); unlimited data with limited budget points toward fine-tuning being more viable, since the expensive part (compute and expertise) is the actual bottleneck, not the training examples.

📋 PM view

Start every "should we fine-tune" conversation by first asking whether RAG alone solves the problem — it's cheaper, faster, and reversible. Only escalate to fine-tuning once RAG has been tried and genuinely falls short on a skill-generalization gap.

09AgentsD5N8Q17

AI Agents — Definitions & Workflows

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. If "agents are programs where LLM outputs control the workflow," does that mean the LLM is effectively writing the program as it runs? What does that imply for testing?
  2. Workflows are predictable; agents are unpredictable. When would a business actually prefer the predictable option?

Best definition (Hugging Face): "AI agents are programs where LLM outputs control the workflow."

What makes a system "agentic"?
1. Multiple LLM calls, chained together
2. Tool use — calls external functions
3. LLM-to-LLM communication
4. A planner LLM coordinating others
5. Autonomy — chooses its own path
WorkflowsAgents
OrderPreset — code orchestrates the sequenceDynamic — LLM decides what happens next
CharacterPredictable, testableUnpredictable, can loop
ExamplesPrompt chaining, routing, evaluator-optimizerOperator, Deep Research, Manus
FIG 9.1 — THREE WORKFLOW PATTERNSAnthropic's taxonomy — predictable, code-orchestrated
What we just learned

Workflows keep the sequence in code — predictable, testable, and easy to reason about — while agents hand the LLM the wheel to decide what happens next, which is genuinely closer to the LLM "writing the program" step by step, and that's precisely why agent behavior is much harder to exhaustively test than workflow behavior. Businesses should prefer workflows whenever the task has a known, repeatable shape (routing a ticket, evaluating output quality) and reserve agents for genuinely open-ended tasks where the path can't be predetermined.

🏗️ Architect view

Default to a workflow, escalate to an agent only when the task's branching is too varied to encode in advance — most "we need an agent" requirements turn out to be a routing or evaluator-optimizer workflow in disguise, which is far easier to test and monitor.

10Tool UseY2K6C43

Tool Use / Function Calling — Demystified

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. If tool use is "just good prompting and an if statement," why wasn't it invented years earlier?
  2. Does an LLM that calls a tool actually "understand" what that tool does — or is it pattern-matching from the prompt?
"Tool use sounds magical. It's actually just good prompting and an IF statement."
FIG 10.1 — HOW TOOL USE ACTUALLY WORKSfive plain steps
What we just learned

The mechanism really is that simple: the LLM never executes anything itself, it just outputs text describing what it wants called, your code checks that against an allow-list and runs it, then feeds the result back in. What's genuinely new isn't the mechanism (prompting plus a conditional has existed for years) — it's that a general-purpose LLM can reliably decide which tool to invoke and with what arguments purely from natural-language instructions, without anyone hand-coding that decision logic. Whether that constitutes "understanding" the tool is philosophically debatable, but functionally it's the same pattern-matching-from-context that underlies everything else the model does.

🎯 CTO view

Because tool use is fundamentally "the LLM's text output triggers your code," the security model is entirely on your side of that boundary — validate and constrain every tool call the same way you'd validate any other untrusted input, since a manipulated prompt could induce an unintended tool call.

Agent Frameworks & Risk Mitigation

  1. More built-in framework features means less code to write, but also less control. When would you actually want less control?
TierExamplesTrade-off
Heavyweight (batteries included)AutoGen (Microsoft), LangGraphMore built-in features, less code, less control
LightweightCrewAI, OpenAI Agents SDKLess built-in, more code, more control

RiskDescription

Unpredictable costsInfinite feedback loops possible; each loop consumes compute $$$; "inference-time scaling" can get expensive fast
Monitoring

See what's happening

Tools like LangSmith track agent interactions so you can understand the actual path taken.

Guardrails

Code-level constraints

Is this action allowed? Has cost exceeded threshold? "Guardrails ensure agents behave with safety and consistency." — OpenAI Agents SDK

What we just learned

Framework weight is a control-vs-speed trade-off, not a quality ranking — a heavyweight framework is the right call when you want to ship fast and accept the framework's built-in patterns, while lightweight or native approaches are right when your use case needs precise control over agent behavior that a batteries-included framework would fight against. Either way, unpredictability and runaway cost are structural risks of agents specifically (not workflows), which is why monitoring and guardrails aren't optional extras — they're the mechanism that makes deploying an agent responsible at all.

🎯 CTO view

Never deploy an agent to production without a hard cost ceiling per run and per time window — an infinite feedback loop is not a hypothetical, it's a predictable failure mode of autonomous systems, and the guardrail needs to exist before the first real user interaction, not after an expensive incident.

12StakeholdersC3T7N26

Cross-Functional Decision Making

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. With potentially 15-20 stakeholders having a viewpoint, how do you avoid decision paralysis while still covering every perspective?
"AI decisions are technical decisions with commercial implications. Everyone needs a seat."
Core teams (driving the decision)Supporting teams (have a viewpoint)
Executive leadership, Data Science, Engineering, Product, Ops, Finance, Sales & MarketingLegal & Compliance, InfoSec, Information Risk Mgmt, HR, Ethics & Responsible AI, Customer Experience/UX, Governance & Audit
🚀 Startup scenario
Even with 3 people, all these hats still need to be worn. Ask explicitly: "Who's wearing the InfoSec hat today?" Ensuring every perspective is considered doesn't require a headcount for every function — it requires deliberately assigning the perspective, even to someone already wearing three other hats.

Decision types that need this team

  1. Which model? (open source vs paid, size, etc.)
  2. Which optimization? (RAG vs fine-tuning vs agents)
  3. Managed AI provider? (Bedrock vs Vertex vs Azure)
  4. Abstraction layer? (LangChain vs CrewAI vs native)
  5. Vector database? (which one for RAG)
What we just learned

The core/supporting split is itself the answer to decision paralysis — core teams actually make the call, supporting teams are consulted for their specific risk lens (legal, security, ethics) rather than all getting equal decision-making weight. The startup version proves the principle scales down: what matters isn't headcount, it's that every relevant perspective gets deliberately assigned to someone, even if one person covers three "hats" at once.

📋 PM view

Time-box supporting-team input (a review window, not an open-ended veto) — the goal is genuine perspective coverage, not consensus from every stakeholder before any decision can move forward.

Active RecallX1D5R92

Test yourself — deep thought questions

Morgan Stanley achieved 98% adoption for their meeting summarizer. What factors drove this success? Could your organization replicate it?
Likely drivers: the tool ran invisibly inside an existing workflow (no new interface to learn), it solved a genuinely painful, time-consuming task (meeting notes), and leadership visibly modeled its use — the same "lead by example" pattern from Topic 2. Replicating it means picking a similarly high-friction, low-risk internal task, embedding the AI into the existing tool rather than adding a new one, and having leadership use and endorse it publicly before asking the wider team to adopt it.
RAG adds context; fine-tuning adds skills. Unlimited budget but limited data — which would you choose? What about unlimited data but limited budget?
Unlimited budget with limited data favors RAG — fine-tuning needs substantial high-quality proprietary examples to work well, and no amount of budget fixes a fundamental data shortage; RAG instead just needs a knowledge base, which is a lower bar to meet. Unlimited data with limited budget still leans toward RAG initially, since fine-tuning's expensive part is compute and specialized expertise, not raw data volume — though at very large data volumes with enough time, a lean, carefully-scoped fine-tuning effort becomes more attractive.
Agents are unpredictable — they choose their own path. In what business contexts is that acceptable? Where is it not?
Acceptable in low-stakes, exploratory, or research-style tasks where the cost of a suboptimal path is low and there's a human review step before any consequential action (e.g. drafting a research summary). Not acceptable in high-stakes, irreversible, or compliance-sensitive actions — financial transactions, medical decisions, customer-facing commitments — where unpredictability directly translates into risk exposure the business can't easily absorb or reverse.
Tool use is "just prompting + if statements." If it's so simple, why wasn't it invented earlier? What changed?
The mechanism (prompt, conditional, execute) isn't new — what's new is that a general-purpose LLM can reliably decide, in natural language, which tool to call and with what arguments across a huge range of tasks without anyone hand-coding that decision tree. Earlier NLP systems couldn't generalize that judgment reliably enough to trust with real actions; large, well-instruction-tuned LLMs crossed a reliability threshold that made the old simple mechanism suddenly trustworthy enough to use in production.
Duolingo saw 40% revenue growth alongside their AI launch. But correlation isn't causation. How would you actually prove AI drove that growth?
A/B testing is the standard tool — randomly holding out a control group without the AI feature and comparing revenue, DAU, and subscription conversion between groups over the same time period isolates the AI's effect from other confounds (seasonality, marketing campaigns, unrelated product changes). Without a controlled comparison, a correlated launch-and-growth story is suggestive but not proof, which is exactly the science-first mindset (Topic 14) the framework recommends applying to every AI claim.
With 15-20+ potential stakeholders, how do you make decisions quickly without losing quality? What trade-offs exist between speed and inclusivity?
Separate "core" decision-makers from "supporting" consulted perspectives (as Topic 12 does) and time-box the supporting teams' input window rather than requiring universal sign-off before any decision proceeds. The trade-off is real: broader inclusion surfaces more risks and builds more buy-in, but past a certain stakeholder count, marginal perspectives add diminishing insight while adding real delay — the framework's answer is structured, time-boxed consultation rather than either extreme (decide alone, or wait for everyone).
If InfoSec says "no data leaves the company," is using frontier models like GPT or Claude automatically out of the question?
Not automatically — enterprise-tier agreements with contractual no-training-on-your-data clauses, data processing agreements, and encryption in transit can satisfy many InfoSec requirements without the data being "used" by the vendor in a way that violates the policy's intent. If InfoSec's policy is stricter than that (literally no data crossing the company boundary under any contractual terms), then direct inference with an in-house, open-source model becomes the only compliant path — this is exactly the kind of trade-off the cross-functional decision framework (Topic 13) exists to work through with InfoSec at the table, not around them.
DeepSeek's training cost ~$5M vs OpenAI's $100M+. If training costs plummet, does fine-tuning become more accessible to smaller companies? What are the implications?
Yes — falling training costs lower the barrier specifically to fine-tuning (which is a smaller training run on top of an existing base model, benefiting from the same cost trends), making the "high risk, high reward" differentiation path in Topic 7 viable for companies that previously couldn't afford it. The competitive implication is that a proprietary fine-tuned model becomes a weaker moat over time as more competitors can afford to build one — pushing sustainable differentiation increasingly toward proprietary data quality and domain expertise rather than the fine-tuning capability itself.
