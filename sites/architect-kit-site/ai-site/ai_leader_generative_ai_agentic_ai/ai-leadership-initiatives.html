Ideation — Three Categories of AI Projects

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. Rank Predictive, Generative, and Agentic AI by risk before reading — then check your instinct against the actual reasoning, not just the label.
  2. Why might an organization's "risk appetite" be a better filter for which category to pursue than technical readiness alone?
Predictive AI

Traditional ML

  • Demand forecasting
  • Churn / purchase prediction
  • Fraud detection
  • Predictive maintenance
  • Workforce analytics

Lower risk, well-understood techniques.

Generative AI

Content creation

  • Text, image, video generation
  • Chatbots & support
  • Copilots for knowledge workers
  • RAG-powered Q&A
  • Synthetic data

Medium risk, proven techniques emerging.

Agentic AI

Autonomous systems

  • Autonomous workers
  • End-to-end process automation
  • Multi-agent ecosystems
  • Self-directed research

Highest risk, highest reward — significant uncertainties and unknowns.

⚡ Tension the source glosses over

The risk ranking (Predictive → Generative → Agentic) is a technology-maturity ranking, not necessarily a business-risk ranking. A well-scoped predictive model deployed into a high-stakes decision (loan denial, medical triage) can carry far more real-world risk than a low-stakes agentic pilot confined to internal research summarization. Don't let "which category" substitute for "what decision does this touch" — the categories describe technical maturity, not blast radius.

What we just learned

The three categories form a genuine maturity curve — Predictive AI has decades of production track record, Generative AI is a few years into proven patterns, Agentic AI is still actively being figured out in production — and that maturity gap is exactly why risk appetite, not just technical capability, should gate which category an organization starts with. A risk-averse organization gains little by attempting Agentic AI first regardless of how technically ready its engineers are, because the category itself carries unresolved uncertainty the org may not be positioned to absorb.

🎯 CTO view

Portfolio the three categories like an investment book — mostly Predictive (proven returns), some Generative (emerging but real), a small deliberate allocation to Agentic (venture-style bets). An all-in bet on any one category, especially Agentic, concentrates risk unnecessarily.

🏗️ Architect view

These categories aren't mutually exclusive in one system — a mature product often layers all three: a predictive model flags anomalies, a generative layer explains them in natural language, and an agentic layer decides whether to auto-remediate or escalate. Architect for that layering from the start rather than assuming you'll pick one category per project forever.

Good Ideas vs Traps

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. Of the four "good idea" criteria — pain point, business driver, proprietary data, measurability — which do you think is hardest to fake convincingly in a pitch meeting?
  2. Count how many of the nine red flags apply to an AI idea you've personally seen proposed. Be honest.

Good ideas should...

1

Speak to a true pain point

Addresses an existing business challenge — not a "nice-to-have."

2

Tick a business driver box

Automation (efficiency), Augmentation (effectiveness), or Differentiation (innovation).

3

Leverage proprietary data

Data others don't have = unfair advantage, world-leading capability.

4

Be easy to measure

Directly measurable impact, a crisp North Star metric, clear ROI demonstration.

Nine red flags

TrapWhat it looks like
Solution looking for a problem"We need an AI strategy" without business context
Change readiness not assessedOrg not prepared for role changes, no buy-in from affected teams
Skills gapsProject needs skills you don't have; hiring timeline not accounted for
R&D risk appetite mismatchOrg can't handle uncertainty; no experimentation budget
Data insufficiencyNot enough data, poor quality, incomplete sets

⚡ Tension the source glosses over

"Leverage proprietary data" and "be easy to measure" can pull in opposite directions. Proprietary, differentiated data is often messy, poorly labeled, and hard to benchmark against anything external — which is exactly what makes it defensible, but also exactly what makes a crisp North Star metric harder to define upfront. The strongest ideas resolve this tension deliberately (define the metric before touching the data), rather than assuming both criteria come for free together.

What we just learned

The four "good idea" criteria and the nine red flags are really the same evaluation from two directions — the criteria describe what a strong idea has, the red flags describe what a weak one is missing or ignoring, and most real pitches land somewhere in between rather than cleanly on one side. The hardest criterion to fake is measurability, precisely because it's the one that gets tested against reality after launch — a vague pain point or an inflated data-advantage claim can survive a pitch meeting, but a promised metric either moves or it doesn't.

🎯 CTO view

Score every proposed idea against all nine red flags explicitly, in writing, before approval — not as gatekeeping theater, but because ideas with three or more unaddressed red flags correlate strongly with the failure patterns in Topic 10's case studies (McDonald's = inadequate testing + no guardrails, essentially red flags 6 and 8 combined).

📋 PM view

"Too broad / vague" is the red flag that kills the most projects slowly rather than fast — a vague goal doesn't get rejected outright, it just never produces a result anyone can point to, and the project quietly loses funding a year later without ever being formally declared a failure.

  2. If 50% of AI jobs were unfilled in 2024, what does that imply about the wisdom of designing a roadmap around a role you haven't hired yet?

Key talent takeaways
Data ≠ Data Science — different hats, different people
50% of AI jobs were unfilled in 2024 — the skills shortage is real
AI Engineers (LLM Engineers) have good supply — prioritize hiring here
Business people with AI experience are a "superpower"
Not every project needs PhD-level data scientists
⚡ Tension the source glosses over

The advice "AI Engineers have good supply, focus hiring here" is true, but it quietly assumes you don't actually need the harder-to-hire Data Scientist role. That's only safe when your use case is closer to Generative AI (applying existing pre-trained models well) than Predictive AI (which genuinely needs statistical modeling expertise) or differentiated Agentic systems. Prioritizing the easy-to-hire role over the right-for-the-task role is exactly the kind of decision that looks efficient in the hiring plan and shows up as a capability gap eighteen months later.

What we just learned

The Data Analyst vs Data Scientist distinction is really a build-vs-apply distinction — analysts extract insight from data that already exists in a known shape, scientists build new statistical models from it — and conflating the two (hiring an analyst expecting scientist-level modeling) is one of the most common, avoidable talent mistakes. The 50%-unfilled statistic isn't just a hiring inconvenience; it's a signal that any roadmap assuming a specific hard-to-fill role (particularly Data Scientist) will be staffed on schedule is building on a coin-flip assumption, which is exactly why Topic 8's uncertainty-management techniques need to extend to talent timelines, not just R&D timelines.

🎯 CTO view

Build the roadmap's Phase 1 resource plan (Topic 7) with two paths for every hard-to-fill role: "if hired on schedule" and "if not — contractor, consultant, or scope reduction." A single-path plan that assumes a Data Scientist appears exactly when needed is optimism, not planning.

📋 PM view

"Business people with AI experience are a superpower" is underrated as a near-term action item — upskilling an existing domain expert in prompt engineering and AI literacy is often faster and cheaper than hiring a new AI Engineer, and that person already has the organizational trust a new hire would need months to build.

Organizational Models for AI

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. Why would most companies start centralized and evolve to hybrid, rather than starting hybrid directly?
FIG 5.1 — THREE ORGANIZATIONAL MODELScentralized → embedded → hybrid
ModelProsCons
CentralizedShared resources, cross-fertilizationBusiness alignment can be challenging
EmbeddedStrong business alignmentLoses shared knowledge & cross-fertilization
HybridBest of both, dotted line to CAIORequires more organizational maturity to run
What we just learned

Companies start centralized because early-stage AI capability is scarce and expensive — pooling it under one Center of Excellence is the only way to get any critical mass of expertise at all. As capability matures and specific business units develop their own AI needs and internal champions, the alignment cost of staying fully centralized starts to outweigh its resource-sharing benefit, which is what pushes the natural evolution toward hybrid: keep a shared core for scarce expertise and infrastructure, but embed people close to the business problems they're actually solving.

🎯 CTO view

Don't design for hybrid on day one if you don't yet have the AI capability to distribute — a premature embedded structure just recreates the "50% unfilled AI jobs" shortage three or four times over instead of once. Centralize first, deliberately plan the migration to hybrid as a milestone, not a starting assumption.

06GovernanceH2B9L57

Governance & Leadership Structures

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. Why would a startup with only 3 people still need something that resembles "governance"? What's the minimum version?
  2. Why split Chief Data Scientist and Chief Data Officer into two roles rather than one?
FIG 6.1 — THREE-TIER GOVERNANCE CADENCEstrategy monthly, execution weekly, work daily
🚀 Startup version
Even 3 people need governance — it's just a daily 5-minute standup and one person deliberately holding the "steering" perspective. The tiers compress, but the cadence discipline (strategic check-in vs execution check-in vs daily blocker check) doesn't disappear.
What we just learned

Governance exists because AI projects combine significant uncertainty, tight cross-team coordination needs, and stakeholder buy-in requirements all at once — remove any one tier and a specific failure mode opens up: no steering committee means strategic drift goes unchecked; no working group means risks surface too late for mitigation; no standup means blockers sit unresolved for days. The Chief Data Scientist/Chief Data Officer split exists for the same reason Data Analyst and Data Scientist are separate roles (Topic 4): data governance (access, quality, lineage, compliance) and data science leadership (model strategy, technical direction) are different disciplines that happen to share a word, and conflating them under one title creates exactly the confusion Topic 4 warned about at the individual-contributor level, just one rung higher.

🎯 CTO view

If your organization only has budget for one senior data leader, hire for whichever gap is currently more dangerous — a Chief Data Officer if your data governance/compliance exposure is the bigger risk, a Chief AI/Data Scientist officer if your technical modeling direction is the bottleneck. Don't default to one title just because it's more common; diagnose which gap is actually costing you.

The AI Roadmap — 5 Phases (+ Phase 0)

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. Which two phases are described as "unique to AI" compared to a standard software project — guess before reading, then check.
FIG 7.1 — PHASE 0 THROUGH PHASE 5phases 2 and 5 (blue) are unique to AI
⚡ Tension the source glosses over

Phase 2 (Research) is flagged as where uncertainty "lives," but Phase 5 (Measure) is where uncertainty gets discovered — a model that seemed successful at the end of R&D can still fail against real-world data distribution once it's actually measured in production. Treating uncertainty as a Phase-2-only problem understates how much can still go wrong after Phase 4's "successful" deployment; the roadmap's loop-back arrow exists precisely because Phase 5 routinely reopens questions Phase 2 thought it had already answered.

What we just learned

Phases 2 and 5 are what make an AI roadmap structurally different from a standard software roadmap — Phase 2 (R&D) exists because you genuinely don't know if a model will hit the required accuracy until you've built and tested it, unlike conventional software where scoping usually predicts feasibility reliably; Phase 5 (Measure) exists because models silently degrade via drift in a way deployed software mostly doesn't. Everything else — planning, building, deploying — maps reasonably well onto familiar project phases, which is exactly why leaders often underestimate an AI project: it looks like a normal five-phase plan with two "extra" phases bolted on, when in reality those two phases are where most of the actual risk and cost live.

🎯 CTO view

Budget and staff Phase 2 and Phase 5 as seriously as Phase 3 (Build) — they're commonly under-resourced because they don't produce visible "shipped" output the way Build and Deploy do, but they're where the project's actual success or failure gets determined.

📋 PM view

Set KPIs in Phase 1, before Phase 2 begins — defining success metrics after seeing R&D results (even informally) invites exactly the kind of retroactive goalpost-moving that undermines the credibility of the eventual ROI claim.

08UncertaintyW9F5N28

Managing Uncertainty

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. What's the difference between "we can't tell you the delivery date" and "we can tell you when we'll know the delivery date"? Why does that distinction actually matter to a stakeholder?
"AI projects are inherently uncertain. As leaders, we must translate between the science team and the executive team."
TechniqueWhat it does
Date for the dateCan't promise delivery date, but can promise "we'll know if this is feasible by [date]." Communicate that externally.
Tentative future datesBest-guess planning dates, clearly marked tentative, dependent on the date-for-the-date resolving.
Contingency timeExtra time built into the roadmap for R&D uncertainty — if it fails, room to pivot.
Plan BA defined mitigation approach ready if Plan A doesn't work.
CommunicationTransparent, regular updates — no surprises.

Five sources of uncertainty to manage

  1. Data — enough? clean? complete?
  2. Landscape — AI changes weekly; new models and frameworks (the OpenAI Agents SDK released during this very briefing)
  3. Model performance — will it hit the required accuracy?
  4. Testing — how do you test unpredictable outputs?
  5. Adoption — will users actually use it?
What we just learned

"We can't commit to a date" sounds like an absence of information and reads as unprofessional; "we'll know if this is feasible by March 15th" is itself a concrete, keepable commitment, just a commitment to a decision point rather than an outcome — and that reframing is the entire trick. It converts an unmanageable promise (a fixed delivery date for a genuinely uncertain R&D outcome) into a manageable one (a fixed date for reducing the uncertainty), which stakeholders can actually plan around even though the underlying uncertainty about the AI itself hasn't gone away.

📋 PM view

"Landscape uncertainty" deserves its own line item in risk registers, not just data and model risk — a competitor's new model release or a framework's deprecation can invalidate technical decisions mid-project in a way that has no equivalent in traditional software, where the underlying platform rarely shifts week to week.

09ChangeC4R7K19

Change Management & Adoption

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. If 70% of AI implementation challenges are people/process issues (BCG), what does that imply about where most companies are actually over-investing?
⚠️ The 70% statistic
70% of AI implementation challenges are people and process issues — not technical (BCG). Yet most AI budgets still skew heavily toward compute, models, and engineering.
Common change challenges
Job displacement fears
Skepticism / AI fatigue (hype backlash)
Misalignment across teams
Ethical bias & explainability concerns
Corporate inertia ("we've always done it this way")
01

Communicate early and often

Frequent transparent updates from the top, candid about positives and negatives, clear on the "why."

02

Upskill & reskill

Invest in education, prepare people for an AI-augmented future, reduce displacement fear through empowerment.

03

Establish AI governance group

Dedicated to ethics, bias, explainability. Ring-fence resources. Tackle concerns from the start.

04

Foster augmentation culture

AI as co-pilot, not replacement. Recognize AI adoption in promotions. Make AI capability part of hiring.

05

Measure and show progress

Metrics that resonate with people. Measure up front and repeatedly. Demonstrate positive change.

06

Be a role model

Use AI openly, share your own success stories, turn skeptics into champions.

⚡ Tension the source glosses over

"Foster augmentation culture" (AI as co-pilot, not replacement) sits uneasily next to the fact that some AI projects genuinely are automation, not augmentation — Klarna's case in Topic 10 literally replaced work equivalent to 700 agents. Promising "co-pilot, not replacement" when the actual project is closer to replacement isn't change management, it's a messaging trap that produces exactly the backlash Klarna experienced when the truth becomes visible. Honest change management sometimes means admitting the project is automation and managing that reality directly, not defaulting to augmentation language because it's more comfortable.

What we just learned

The 70% statistic is the single most consequential number in this whole module — it means the majority of AI project risk sits in a category (people, process) that most organizations' AI budgets and staffing plans systematically under-invest in relative to the technical category (models, compute, engineering) that gets the majority of attention and funding. Every one of the six strategies here is really a variant of the same move: make the change visible, honest, and led from the top, rather than treating adoption as something that happens automatically once the technology works.

🎯 CTO view

If your AI project budget doesn't allocate a meaningful percentage explicitly to change management and communication — not as an afterthought line item but as a planned workstream with its own owner — you're statistically underinvesting in the category most likely to determine whether the project succeeds at all.

📋 PM view

Decide upfront, honestly, whether a project is Automation, Augmentation, or Differentiation (the framework from earlier modules) before writing the change-management messaging — messaging that promises augmentation for a project that's actually automation is the specific mechanism that produced the Klarna backlash.

10Case StudiesM1T6W52

Case Studies — Successes & Failures

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. Klarna's AI genuinely worked (700 agents' worth of capacity). Why did it still generate backlash? What does that tell you about the limits of technical success alone?
✅ Morgan Stanley — The Gold Standard
Meeting summarizer for advisors · 98% adoption · 10–15 hrs/week saved per advisor

Success factors: rigorous evaluation framework, top experts involved to validate, and messaging that framed the tool as making "every advisor as smart as the smartest expert" — an augmentation story, told honestly, that people wanted to believe because it was also true.

✅ Telstra — Staged Rollout Success
Agentic AI platform for customer service · 80–90% adoption

Success factors: phased implementation, early adopters became champions, customer service improvements were celebrated publicly rather than downplayed.

⚠️ Klarna — Mixed Messaging
AI assistant for customer support · equivalent to 700 full-time agents

What went wrong: messaging focused on job displacement, done alongside a hiring freeze, producing backlash from employees and the public. Lesson: how you communicate matters as much as the result — the technology succeeded; the narrative around it didn't.

❌ McDonald's — The Tale of Woe
AI drive-thru assistant · 270 Chicken McNuggets ordered · project cancelled

What went wrong: inadequate testing, no human-in-the-loop guardrails, viral embarrassment. Lessons: testing AI systems is different from testing conventional software; guardrails are essential; high-risk, customer-facing decisions need a human checkpoint.

What we just learned

These four cases map cleanly onto the module's two big themes: Morgan Stanley and Telstra succeeded on change management (honest framing, staged rollout, celebrated wins), while McDonald's failed on the technical/testing side (Topic 3's "unrealistic expectations" and "model drift ignored" red flags, plus zero guardrails). Klarna is the most instructive case precisely because it inverts the usual failure story — the AI worked exactly as intended, at real scale, and it still produced a public relations crisis, which proves conclusively that technical success and organizational success are separate achievements that both have to be earned, not one guaranteeing the other.

🎯 CTO view

McDonald's is the canonical argument for mandatory human-in-the-loop review on any customer-facing, high-visibility deployment during initial rollout — the cost of a a staged rollout with human oversight is trivial compared to the cost of a viral failure that gets the entire project cancelled.

📋 PM view

Klarna's lesson generalizes: never let the change-management narrative get finalized independently from the actual business decision (a hiring freeze, in this case) it sits alongside — a technically accurate but tone-deaf combination of "efficiency win" messaging next to visible job impact is a predictable, avoidable failure of coordination between comms and the underlying business action.

Active RecallF7L9K38

Test yourself — deep thought questions

Morgan Stanley achieved 98% adoption. What's the equivalent "adoption metric" for your AI project, and how would you actually measure it?
The right metric depends on whether the tool is embedded (like Morgan Stanley's summarizer, invisible inside the existing workflow — measure usage rate per eligible user per week) or a standalone destination (measure return-usage rate, not just first-use). The deeper point: adoption alone isn't sufficient — Morgan Stanley paired 98% adoption with a rigorous evaluation framework proving the outputs were actually good, so any adoption metric needs a paired quality metric, or high adoption of a mediocre tool just means people gave up checking its work.
Would you choose a project with a clear metric but moderate impact, or high impact but vague metrics? Why?
Clear metric, moderate impact, especially for a first AI initiative — an unmeasurable "high impact" claim can't be defended when budget renewal comes around, and the Topic 3 red flags ("too broad/vague") specifically warn against exactly this trap. A moderate, provable win builds the organizational trust and change-management credibility (Topic 9) needed to attempt the higher-impact, harder-to-measure project next — sequencing matters more than either single choice.
Klarna's messaging focused on job displacement and faced backlash. How would you frame the same underlying result differently?
The honest reframing isn't spin — it's separating the technology announcement from the workforce decision and handling each with its own communication track: announce the capability and customer-experience improvement on its own merits, and handle the hiring-freeze/staffing decision through a separate, direct conversation with affected teams rather than letting the AI announcement double as the layoff announcement. The mistake wasn't claiming an efficiency win; it was letting the efficiency win and the human cost collapse into a single, tone-deaf message.
McDonald's ordered 270 chicken nuggets. What's your worst-case scenario if your AI project fails, and what guardrails would prevent it?
The exercise itself matters more than any specific answer: name the worst plausible failure mode explicitly (a wrong high-value transaction, a leaked confidential document, a customer-facing hallucination going viral) before launch, not after. The generalizable guardrail from McDonald's failure is a human-in-the-loop checkpoint for any action above a defined risk or cost threshold, combined with adversarial testing (deliberately trying to break the system) before any customer-facing rollout — not just testing that it works under normal conditions.
50% of AI jobs were unfilled in 2024. If you can't hire a data scientist, how would you still deliver an AI project?
Several viable paths: lean toward Generative AI use cases that need an AI/LLM Engineer (good supply, per Topic 4) more than a Data Scientist (scarce supply); use a fractional consultant or agency for the specific modeling work rather than a full-time hire; or upskill an existing analyst or domain expert with targeted statistics training for a narrowly-scoped predictive task. The deeper lesson is to let talent availability inform which project category (Topic 1) you pursue first, rather than picking the project first and then discovering the required role doesn't exist in the market at any price you can pay.
If you're a startup with 3 people, how do you implement "governance" without bureaucracy? What's the minimum viable governance structure?
Minimum viable governance is a daily standup covering yesterday/today/blockers, plus a weekly moment where someone deliberately asks "what would InfoSec/Legal/Ethics say about this if we had those people?" — the goal is ensuring every perspective gets considered, not creating committees. The three-tier cadence from Topic 6 (strategic, execution, daily) can compress to one person wearing multiple hats across a single weekly conversation rather than three separate meetings, as long as all three time horizons (strategy, risk/milestones, blockers) get deliberately addressed somewhere.
The AI landscape changed during this very briefing (OpenAI Agents SDK released mid-course). How do you build "landscape change" into your roadmap?
Treat landscape uncertainty as its own named risk category in the roadmap (alongside data, model performance, testing, and adoption uncertainty from Topic 8), with an explicit review checkpoint — for example, revisit the technology/vendor choice at the end of Phase 2 rather than assuming the tooling decision made in Phase 1 will still be optimal by Phase 4. Building in contingency time (Topic 8) isn't just for R&D risk — it also buys room to absorb a genuinely better tool or model becoming available mid-project without blowing the whole timeline.
70% of AI challenges are people issues. If that's true, why do most companies still focus their AI investments on technology, not people?
Technology investment is easier to specify, procure, and demonstrate progress on — buying GPU capacity or an API contract produces a visible line item and a concrete deliverable, while change management produces harder-to-quantify outcomes (trust, adoption willingness, reduced fear) that don't fit neatly into a procurement process or a Gantt chart. There's also an incentive mismatch: technical teams and vendors are naturally positioned to sell and justify more technology spend, while nobody has an equivalent commercial incentive pushing organizations to over-invest in change management — which is exactly why leadership has to deliberately correct for that imbalance rather than assuming the market will self-correct it.
