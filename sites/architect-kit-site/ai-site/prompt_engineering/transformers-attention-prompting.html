01Get the paper right, and know what it replaced

Two small fixes first. The paper is "Attention Is All You Need," Vaswani et al., 2017 — the lecturer says "Keswani." The lead author is Ashish Vaswani. Worth knowing because you will cite it, and citing it wrong in a room with an ML person is an unnecessary own goal.

Second: the authors were mostly at Google Brain and Google Research, with one co-author at the University of Toronto. Minor, but "these Google scientists" is close enough.

Now the part that matters. What did the Transformer actually replace, and why did it win? The lecture says RNNs and LSTMs were slower. True, and the reason is specific and worth holding onto.

A recurrent network reads a sentence one word at a time, carrying a hidden state forward. Word 50 cannot be computed until word 49 is done. That is inherently serial, and a GPU with thousands of cores sits mostly idle. The Transformer's insight was to let every position look at every other position simultaneously. Training that used to take weeks became days.

So the 2017 win was not primarily "attention understands language better." It was parallelism during training. That distinction matters: it explains why the architecture scaled when everything before it plateaued, and it sets up the irony in §09 — generation is still serial, so the thing that made training fast did nothing for inference.

Note · The other thing the paper did

It removed convolution and recurrence entirely and showed you could still win. That's why the title is a joke — "attention is all you need" was a claim that the components everyone thought were essential weren't. Papers with cheeky titles are usually making a subtraction argument. Reading it that way tells you what to look for.

They made training parallel across the sequence, so GPUs could actually be saturated — and they let any two positions interact in one step instead of many.

Both halves matter. Parallelism is why the architecture could absorb enormous data and compute. One-hop long-range interaction is why it handles long documents where an RNN's memory of the opening had already faded.

Rule: when an architecture wins, check whether it won on quality or on hardware fit. Very often it's hardware fit, and that predicts what will break next.

Because in this field, speed converts into quality through scale. If training a model of size X takes a month, you can't afford to try 10X. Once the same hardware trains 10X in a month, you find out what 10X does — and it turned out that quality kept improving predictably with more parameters and more data, far past where anyone expected a plateau.

So the causal chain is: parallel architecture → GPUs saturated → bigger models feasible → scaling laws discovered → the entire industry pivots to scale. None of that was in the 2017 paper, which was a translation paper with modest models. The architecture was a key that fitted a lock nobody had described yet.

The practical version for you: when you evaluate a new technique, ask whether it removes a bottleneck or improves a result. Bottleneck removals compound. Result improvements don't.

Transfers: the same shape as columnar storage, SSDs, or containerisation — none made any single query, read, or deploy fundamentally smarter. They removed a constraint, and the constraint was what capped everything downstream.

02Correct the architecture: your models are decoder-only

This is the correction the whole module depends on, so it gets said plainly.

GPT, Claude, Gemini and Llama do not have an encoder. They have no encoder-decoder cross-attention. The entire left-hand column of the diagram the lecture explains — the input embedding for the source, the encoder self-attention, the encoder feedforward stack, and the arrow crossing into the decoder — is not present in any of the models it names.

Why does the 2017 paper have both halves? Because it was a machine translation paper. Translation genuinely has two sequences: an English source and a French target. They're different languages, different lengths, and every target word may depend on any source word. So you encode the source into a representation, then decode the target while attending back to it. Two sequences, two stacks.

A modern LLM does not have two sequences. It has one. Your prompt and the model's answer are the same stream of tokens, processed by the same stack of layers, with the same operation. There is no line in the middle. The model isn't reading your input and then writing an output — it is continuing a single document that happens to have your text at the start.

The lecture also names BERT alongside GPT as sharing this backend. BERT is encoder-only — the opposite half. So the two examples given are architecturally opposites, and neither is the encoder-decoder being drawn.

Trap · Why this error is expensive, not just wrong

Believing there's an encoder makes you think your prompt gets "read and understood" into a separate representation, and then the answer gets "written" from it. That mental model predicts a boundary — and a boundary implies protection. It's exactly the intuition that makes prompt injection feel like a bug that could be patched. There is no boundary to protect. That's why injection is structural.

I'd say the architecture they're describing doesn't exist in the model we're calling — and then I'd take their idea seriously, because the instinct is right even though the mechanism is wrong.

The correction: decoder-only models have one stream. Our instructions, the retrieved document, and the model's answer all live in the same sequence, processed identically. There is no second channel to put untrusted content into. Nothing at the model level separates instructions from data.

The instinct worth keeping: they're asking for privilege separation, which is the right thing to want. We just can't get it from the model. We get it from the layers around the model — the tool allow-list, the least-privilege database role, the human approval gate on outbound actions. That's where the boundary actually lives.

What we can do inside the prompt is weaker but not worthless: delimit untrusted spans clearly, put them last, and instruct the model to treat them as data. That reduces success rate. It does not create a boundary, and I'd be careful not to let anyone leave the meeting thinking it did.

Transfers: when someone proposes a control based on a wrong mental model, fix the model and keep the requirement. The requirement is usually sound; only the mechanism was imaginary.

Because of what you can train on, and what you can do afterwards.

Training signal. A decoder-only model predicts the next token at every single position in the sequence. A 1,000-token document gives you 1,000 training examples. BERT-style masked training only predicts the small fraction of tokens you masked — typically 15% — so you extract far less learning per token of data. When data and compute are the constraint, that ratio decides the race.

Generality. A decoder-only model can do classification, extraction, summarisation, translation and generation, because all of them can be written as "continue this text." An encoder-only model needs a task-specific head bolted on and fine-tuned per task. One general model beat many specialised ones — the same story as general-purpose CPUs beating specialised chips for most workloads.

The genuine loss is real, though: bidirectional context is better for pure understanding tasks. Which is why encoder models are still the right choice for embeddings — retrieval, semantic search, classification at scale. Your RAG pipeline almost certainly uses an encoder model for the embeddings and a decoder model for the answer. Both families are in your stack; they just do different jobs.

Transfers: the architecture that extracts more signal per unit of data wins when data is the bottleneck, even if it's worse per parameter. Efficiency of learning beats efficiency of representation.

03The buried lede: the mask is not an anti-cheating rule

The line

"It uses a mask to prevent cheating. And it can't look at the future words."

Five seconds, then the lecture moves to the next box. That mask is the single most consequential design decision in the entire architecture, and almost everything you have learned about prompting descends from it.

Here is what the causal mask actually is. When the model computes the representation of token 7, it is allowed to attend to tokens 1 through 7. It is forbidden to attend to tokens 8 onward. Mechanically, the attention scores for future positions are set to negative infinity before the softmax, so they come out as exactly zero. Every position sees the past and its own position. Never the future.

The lecture frames this as fairness during training — don't let the model peek at the answer. That's true and it's the smallest part of it. The mask is what makes the model causal, and causality is a structural property with five consequences you have already been living with.

One: a token's representation never changes once computed. Token 7's meaning depends only on tokens 1-7. Adding token 8 cannot alter it. Nothing that comes later can reach back.

Two: that is exactly why the KV cache works. If earlier representations were mutable, you'd recompute the whole sequence for every new token. Because they're frozen, you compute each token once and keep it. Generation goes from quadratic-per-token to linear.

Three: that is why prompt caching exists. A vendor can cache the processed state of your stable prefix and reuse it across requests — because that state provably cannot depend on whatever you append later. The 90% discount on cached prefixes is not a billing promotion. It is a consequence of a triangle. And it is why the ordering rule from the first module — stable content first, volatile content last — is a mechanical fact rather than a style preference.

Four: it is why prefill parallelises and decoding does not. Reading your prompt, all positions are known, so every token's representation is computed at once. Writing the answer, token N+1 does not exist until token N is chosen. Same architecture, opposite behaviour.

Five: it is why there is no backspace. The model cannot revise an earlier token because nothing downstream is permitted to influence anything upstream. Autoregression is not a policy. It is the mask.

Good · The rule this hands you

Anything you want the model to consider when interpreting a piece of text must appear before that text, or be re-stated after it. A token's representation is built without any knowledge of what follows. Put the framing first, put the raw material after it, and if the instruction is critical, repeat it at the end where it can attend to everything.

I'd say build it — exact-match caching is genuinely valuable for repeated queries — but point out they're leaving the bigger win on the table, and the two are complementary rather than alternatives.

Their cache only fires on identical whole prompts. In a system where every request has a different user message, the hit rate is near zero no matter how good the cache is.

Prefix caching fires on identical beginnings, which is nearly every request, because the system prompt, schema and examples are the same every time. That works precisely because the mask guarantees the prefix's computed state cannot depend on the suffix. It's the vendor's cache, not ours, and we enable it by ordering the template correctly and marking the boundary.

So the advice is: do both, but do the ordering audit first because it's a template change rather than a new component. And measure the cached-token counts in the API response afterwards — if the cache-read number is zero, something in the "stable" prefix isn't stable, and a timestamp or a session ID has crept in near the top.

Transfers: before building a cache, find out what the layer below you already caches and what invalidates it. Most of the time the win is in cooperating with an existing cache rather than adding one.

Because the question can see the document — the restriction runs one way, and you've put the question in the position that benefits.

What you lose is subtler. The document's own token representations were computed with no idea what would be asked. Every token in that document has been encoded "generically". When the question finally arrives, it attends back over those generic representations and pulls out what it needs. That works, and it's how most RAG functions.

What you'd gain by putting the question first is that every document token is then encoded while the question is already in context — the model can weight what's relevant as it reads. The effect is real but usually modest, and it costs you the prompt cache, because a question at the top breaks the shared prefix.

Which gives the practical answer most teams should use: stable framing first, document next, specific question last, and repeat any critical instruction after the document. You get the cache and the attention.

Transfers: in any one-directional pipeline, ask which stage benefits from knowing about the other. Then either reorder, or pass the information twice. Repeating the instruction is the cheap version of reordering.

The mask freezes earlier token states, which lets vendors cache your stable prefix, which is why putting variable content at the top of a template can multiply your input bill by five or six.

Nothing about that is a pricing policy you could negotiate. It's arithmetic on a triangle.

Rule: stable content first, volatile content last. Now you know it's mechanical, not a convention.

04Separate tokenization from embedding — the lecture merges them

The lecture says the input embedding layer is "responsible for converting to a token ID." That's two different steps merged into one, and keeping them apart matters because they have different properties and different failure modes.

Tokenization is a fixed lookup table. Text goes in, integers come out. It is not a neural network, it has no parameters that were learned by gradient descent, and it does not change during training. This is where the 4-characters-per-token rule and the Indic tokenizer tax live.

The embedding layer is a learned table with one vector per vocabulary entry. Integer goes in, a vector of a few thousand numbers comes out. This is where meaning starts to exist. Similar words end up with similar vectors, and that is learned, not designed.

Those vectors then enter what interpretability researchers call the residual stream — a running total that every layer reads from and writes back into. The lecture calls this "add & norm" and describes it as a normalisation step. It's more than that: the residual connection is the highway. Each attention block and each feedforward block reads the current state, computes an update, and adds it. Nothing overwrites; everything accumulates.

That framing is useful because it explains what a deep model is doing. Layer 1 has a vague, mostly-lexical representation. By layer 40 the same position holds something far richer, built by forty rounds of "read the stream, add what you know." The model doesn't transform your prompt into an answer. It accumulates meaning at every position until the last position is confident enough to name the next token.

Sketch 4 · Why "add & norm" is the most underrated box on the diagram

Embedding is learned; tokenization is not. The tokenizer is a fixed vocabulary file built before training and frozen forever.

It matters because the two have different consequences. The tokenizer determines your cost and your context budget — and it's why Tamil costs multiples of English. The embedding determines whether the model has any useful meaning for a token. A rare token gets fragmented by the tokenizer and its fragments have weaker learned embeddings, so obscure terms are hit twice.

Rule: tokenizer problems are cost problems. Embedding problems are quality problems. Different fixes.

Serial composition. Each layer can only use what's already in the stream, so a computation requiring three dependent steps needs at least three layers. Depth is the number of sequential reasoning steps available inside a single forward pass.

That gives you a genuinely useful prediction. A problem needing more dependent steps than the model has layers cannot be solved in one forward pass, no matter how good the model is. The only way to get more steps is to generate tokens — which restarts the whole stack with the intermediate result now written into the context.

Which is, mechanically, why chain-of-thought works. It isn't a psychological trick that makes the model "try harder". It converts a depth limit into a length limit. You can't add layers; you can add tokens. Each generated token buys you another full pass through every layer.

Transfers: when a system has a fixed pipeline depth, the way to compute something deeper is to loop the output back to the input. Same reason multi-pass compilers exist.

05Understand position — and why the method changed

The lecture's explanation of positional encoding is correct and incomplete. It says position is added so the model knows word order: "I" is first, "love" second, "transformers" third. Right. But it doesn't say why that's necessary, and the why is the interesting part.

Attention has no built-in sense of order. If you shuffle the tokens, the attention computation produces the same set of relationships — it's a bag of items, not a sequence. "The bank approved the loan" and "The loan approved the bank" would be indistinguishable. Position has to be injected deliberately, or the architecture literally cannot tell word order exists.

The 2017 paper injected it with fixed sinusoidal patterns added to the embeddings. Elegant, and largely gone. Most modern models use RoPE — rotary position embeddings — which encodes position by rotating the query and key vectors by an angle proportional to position. The consequence is that attention between two tokens depends on their relative distance rather than their absolute slots.

Why you should care, in one sentence: this is why context windows can be extended after training, and why models get worse past the length they were trained on. Techniques for stretching a model's context — position interpolation, RoPE scaling, YaRN — all work by manipulating those rotation angles. When a vendor announces a longer context window, this is usually what changed, and it's why "supports 200k tokens" and "is good at 200k tokens" are separate claims you must test separately.

Sketch 5 · The reason a context-window number is a marketing figure until you test it
Warn · The effective window is shorter than the advertised one

Independent long-context evaluations consistently find models degrade well before their stated limit — retrieval of a single fact holds up much longer than reasoning over many scattered facts. Plan your context budget against measured performance on your task, and treat the advertised number as a hard ceiling rather than a working range.

Three objections, and I'd lead with the one finance cares about because it's the least arguable.

Cost. RAG sends maybe 4,000 relevant tokens. The manual is several hundred thousand. That's a two-order-of-magnitude increase in input cost on every single request, forever — and it also destroys prefix caching, because a manual is stable but the retrieved-versus-full decision changes the whole shape of the prompt. This alone usually ends the conversation.

Latency. Prefill scales with prompt length. Time-to-first-token goes from under a second to several. Users feel that immediately.

Quality, which is the counter-intuitive one. More context is not more understanding. A long prompt full of near-miss distractors measurably degrades precision, and degradation near the advertised limit is well documented. Retrieval with good ranking often beats a full dump, not just ties it on cost.

Where I'd agree with them: bigger windows make retrieval easier. We can stop agonising over the top 3 chunks and send 20. That's a real simplification and worth taking. The proposal is right in spirit and wrong in degree — and I'd offer to run both against the golden set so the decision is a number rather than an argument.

Transfers: when a limit is raised, ask whether the limit was ever the binding constraint. Usually the constraint was the cost of approaching it, and that didn't move.

Because the attention operation is order-invariant. It computes relationships between items in a set. Shuffle the tokens and you get the same relationships back. Without an explicit position signal the model cannot distinguish "the bank approved the loan" from "the loan approved the bank."

Rule: whenever a mechanism is invariant to something you care about, that information must be supplied separately. Position here, units in physics code, timezone in timestamps.
PART II

Attention, mechanically

−

The lecture says "each word looks at the other words to understand the context." That sentence is doing a great deal of work. This part unpacks what "looks at" means, what it costs, and where the model's parameters actually live — which is not where you'd guess.

06Read attention as a soft lookup

"Each word looks at the other words" is a metaphor. Here is the mechanism, and it will feel familiar because you have implemented something like it many times.

Every token produces three vectors from its current state in the residual stream. A query — what am I looking for? A key — what do I offer to anyone looking? A value — what do I actually hand over if chosen? All three are produced by learned weight matrices.

Then: take one token's query, dot it against every other token's key. High dot product means high relevance. Softmax those scores into weights summing to 1. Use the weights to take a weighted average of everyone's values. Add the result to the residual stream.

So it is a dictionary lookup where the match is a matter of degree. A hash map returns the value at one exact key. Attention returns a blend of all values, weighted by how well each key matched the query. Nothing is retrieved absolutely; everything is retrieved partially.

Two practical consequences. Relevance is content-based and learned — the model decides what "relevant" means, and that is why rewording a prompt genuinely changes what gets retrieved, rather than merely sounding different. And the softmax means attention is a zero-sum budget: weights must sum to 1, so more attention on one token is necessarily less on another. Adding irrelevant material to a prompt does not just waste tokens. It competes.

07Understand why it is multi-head

The lecture says "multi-head attention, which we call self-attention layer" as though the two terms were interchangeable. They're not, and the difference is the useful bit.

Self-attention describes what the tokens attend to — themselves, the same sequence, rather than some other sequence. Multi-head describes how many independent attention operations run side by side in one layer. A model might run 32 or 96 heads per layer, each with its own query, key and value matrices, each producing its own weighted blend. The results are concatenated and mixed.

Why bother? Because one attention pattern per layer would force the model to choose. In "The loan officer approved her claim because she met the criteria," the token she needs to know its grammatical role, its referent, the tense, and the clause structure — several different relationships, all at once. One softmax can only concentrate on so much. Multiple heads let different relationships be tracked in parallel and combined.

Interpretability research has found that heads do specialise, and often in recognisable ways: heads that attend to the previous token, heads that track syntactic dependencies, heads that resolve pronouns, and — most importantly for you — induction heads that find an earlier occurrence of the current pattern and copy what followed it. That last family is the closest thing we have to a mechanical explanation of few-shot prompting, and §13 comes back to it.

08Price the quadratic — and know what it actually costs you

"Each word looks at the other words." Count that. With n tokens, you compute n × n attention scores per head per layer. Double the context and you quadruple that work. This is the famous quadratic cost, and the lecture never mentions it.

But here is where most explanations go wrong, and it's worth getting right because it changes what you optimise. The quadratic term is not usually your dominant compute cost. Alongside attention, every layer runs large matrix multiplications on each token that scale linearly with length but have a big constant. In a large model, the linear term dominates the quadratic one until context lengths in the tens of thousands. So at 4,000 tokens, attention is a minority of your FLOPs.

What the quadratic really costs you is memory and bandwidth. The KV cache grows linearly with every token and must be re-read on every single decode step. At long context you become memory-bandwidth-bound: the GPU spends its time hauling cached keys and values rather than computing. Throughput collapses long before the FLOP count becomes the problem.

And there is a compounding effect nobody mentions for Indian-language products. Tamil costs roughly 4.7× the tokens of English for the same content. That is 4.7× on everything linear — and on the attention term, 4.7 squared, about 22×. The tokenizer tax is not a flat multiplier. It has a quadratic component.

By attacking the constant factor and the memory traffic rather than the exponent, and by not doing full attention everywhere.

09Locate the KV cache, and see why it decides your serving cost

The lecture's translation example shows the decoder being fed "start", then "start Je", then "start Je suis" — the whole sequence re-entering the model each time. Read literally, that means recomputing everything at every step, and it would make generation impossibly slow.

It doesn't happen, because of the mask. Earlier tokens' keys and values can never change, so you compute them once and keep them. That store is the KV cache, and it is the single most important object in LLM serving that no tutorial mentions.

The arithmetic is exact and worth being able to do on a whiteboard. Cache size = 2 (keys and values) × layers × key-value dimension × sequence length × batch size × bytes per number. For a large model at long context this is not a rounding error — it can exceed the size of the weights themselves.

That single fact explains a pile of things you may have noticed. Why long conversations get slower, not just pricier. Why providers cap concurrent sessions. Why grouped-query attention exists — sharing key-value heads across query heads cuts this memory by the sharing ratio, often 8×, for almost no quality loss. And why, if you self-host, your GPU sizing is dominated by how many concurrent long conversations you expect, not by the weights.

Warn · The capacity model people get wrong

Teams size self-hosted deployments on weights and average token throughput, then discover that thirty users in long sessions exhaust GPU memory while the compute sits idle. Capacity is concurrent context, not requests per second. Model it as: how many simultaneous conversations, at what average length, in what precision — then check that against KV cache memory before you check anything else.

The causal mask. Because a token can only attend to earlier positions, its key and value vectors depend only on what came before it — so appending new tokens can never change them. They are safe to store and reuse.

Remove the mask and you have a bidirectional model, where adding a token at the end changes the representation of every earlier token. Nothing is cacheable. That is one reason encoder-only models are not used for generation.

Rule: caching is always downstream of immutability. Find what can't change, and that's what you can store.

Four things, and the first one reframes the whole exercise.

How many concurrent conversations, at what length? Not requests per second — that's the wrong unit. Fifty users each holding a 20,000-token session is a completely different machine from five hundred users sending 500-token one-shots at the same request rate. The spec sheet talks about throughput; my constraint is concurrent context.

Does this checkpoint use grouped-query attention, and at what ratio? An 8:1 ratio is roughly an 8× reduction in cache memory. That can be the difference between three GPUs and one, and it's usually buried in a config file rather than the marketing page.

What's our precision, for weights and for the cache separately? People quantise weights and forget the cache can be quantised too. It's often the larger number.

What's the eviction policy when memory fills? Because it will. Does a long session get dropped, queued, or silently truncated? Silent truncation is the worst answer and it's a common default — the user sees the model "forget" the start of their conversation with no error anywhere.

Then I'd load-test at our realistic session length rather than at 512 tokens, because that's where all the pleasant benchmark numbers come from.

Transfers: for any stateful service, capacity is measured in concurrent state, not in request rate. Connection pools, websockets, and this all fail the same way when sized on throughput alone.

10Find out where the parameters actually live

The lecture calls the feedforward network "a simple feedforward neural network... applying a deeper logic" and moves on in one sentence. Attention gets several minutes. That allocation is exactly backwards from where the model's capacity sits.

In a standard Transformer layer, the attention block holds roughly 4 × d² parameters and the feedforward block holds roughly 8 × d², where d is the model's width. Two thirds of every layer is feedforward. In a 70-billion-parameter model, around 47 billion of those parameters are in the part the lecture describes in one clause.

What is that part doing? The current best understanding is that the feedforward layers act like a key-value memory. The first matrix projects the residual stream up into a much wider space where individual dimensions correspond to recognisable features — patterns, facts, concepts. A non-linearity keeps the ones that fire. The second matrix projects back down, writing the associated content into the stream.

So the division of labour is clean and worth memorising: attention moves information between positions. Feedforward layers add information from the model's stored knowledge. Attention is the routing; feedforward is the memory. When a model "knows" that Ottawa is the capital of Canada, that fact is far more likely to live in feedforward weights than in an attention head.

That framing has a practical payoff. If your prompt fails because the model didn't connect two things in the context, that's a routing problem — reorder, clarify, shorten, put things closer together. If it fails because the model didn't know something, that's a memory problem, and no amount of rewording fixes it. You need retrieval. Knowing which failure you have is worth more than any prompt template.

Note · Mixture of experts, in one paragraph

Many current large models replace the single feedforward block with many parallel "expert" blocks and a router that activates only a couple per token. Never compare two models on headline parameter count alone.

Because retrieving from context and retrieving from weights are different operations with different reliability.

Context retrieval is attention — a soft lookup over material that is physically present, exact, and copyable. The model can attend to the token and reproduce it. Weight retrieval is a reconstruction from distributed parameters that were shaped by millions of documents; it produces something plausible in the right shape, and it has no mechanism for signalling that the reconstruction failed.

So supplying the document converts an unreliable reconstruction into a reliable copy. That is the entire mechanical case for RAG, and it explains why grounding is the single biggest lever on hallucination — you're changing which subsystem answers the question.

It also predicts the limits. Grounding doesn't help if retrieval fetched the wrong document, if the answer requires combining many scattered facts (that's routing, and it's harder), or if the model needs background knowledge to interpret what it read. Those failures survive perfect retrieval.

Transfers: whenever a system can answer from a cache or from a computation, find out which one fired. They have completely different failure modes, and monitoring usually only watches one.

Attention moves information between positions. Feedforward layers add information from what the model learned during training.

Rule: the "turn structure" you see in a chat API is tokens the SDK adds for you. Know what it inserts, especially if you ever bypass it.

Because the task is rarely just translation. In a real product you want the translation to preserve formatting, respect a glossary of product terms, keep a brand register, handle a mix of English and Hindi in one sentence, and leave account numbers untouched. A dedicated translation model does one thing excellently and none of the rest.

A general model does the translation slightly worse and everything around it much better, because you can specify all of that in the prompt. And you avoid operating a second model with its own deployment, evaluation and lifecycle.

Where the specialised model still wins clearly: very high volume with a narrow, stable task, where cost per token dominates and the quality bar is well-defined. That's a genuine case, and it's the same shape as the routing argument — cheap specialist for the bulk, general model for the awkward residue.

Transfers: general tools beat specialised ones when the real job has ragged edges, which it usually does. Specialists win on volume and narrowness. Ask which one your workload actually is.

12Place instructions where the mask lets them work

Now the payoff. The follow-up lecture says rearranging a question "can dramatically change the output" and stops there. Here's the mechanical version, and it produces a template you can use unchanged.

Two facts do all the work. The mask: a token's representation is computed without any knowledge of what follows it. The softmax budget: attention weights sum to 1, so everything in the prompt competes.

Together they give you four rules. Framing goes first, so the document is encoded while the role and task are already in context. The document goes in the middle, clearly delimited. The specific question goes last, where it can attend over everything. Critical constraints get restated after the document, because that's the only position from which they can attend to the material they constrain.

That last one looks like redundancy and isn't. An instruction placed before a 5,000-token document is competing with 5,000 tokens for attention at the moment the answer is generated. Twenty tokens restating it at the end is one of the highest-value trades in prompting.

And note the happy accident: this ordering is also the cache-optimal ordering from the first module. Stable framing at the top is exactly what the prefix cache wants. The attention-optimal layout and the cost-optimal layout are the same layout, because both descend from the same triangle.

13Explain few-shot properly: it is pattern completion, not learning

The follow-up lecture says few-shot prompting works because it "guides the model's internal attention process." That's directionally right and vague enough to be unfalsifiable. Here's the mechanism, and it changes how you write examples.

Interpretability work identified a circuit called an induction head. It does two things. First, find an earlier place in the context where the current pattern already occurred. Second, copy whatever came next. Two attention heads in successive layers, composed. That's it.

Now look at a few-shot prompt through that lens. You give three examples of input → output. The model reaches the fourth input, and the induction circuit finds the earlier positions with the same shape and copies the structure that followed them. Nothing is learned. No weight changes. It is pattern completion over your context.

"In-context learning" is a misleading name that has stuck. It sounds like training. It isn't — the model is the same after your request as before it, and the effect vanishes the moment the context clears.

Three practical consequences fall out immediately. Format consistency matters more than example quality, because the circuit copies structure. Three examples in three different layouts teach nothing to copy. Examples must be self-consistent: if two of your examples handle the same edge case differently, you've taught ambiguity. And the last example is the strongest, because it's nearest and attention weakens with distance — so put your most representative one last, not first.

14Explain chain-of-thought from the residual stream

The lecture groups chain-of-thought with few-shot as things that "guide the model's internal attention." They work for genuinely different reasons, and conflating them means you'll apply the wrong one.

Few-shot exploits attention circuits copying a pattern that is already in the context. Chain-of-thought exploits depth, and specifically the fact that you can't add layers but you can add tokens.

Recall §04: each layer reads the residual stream and adds to it, so a computation needing three dependent steps needs at least three layers. Depth is your budget of sequential reasoning steps per forward pass, and it is fixed by the model. If a problem needs more steps than the model has layers, one forward pass cannot solve it. Full stop.

Generating a token restarts everything. The intermediate result is now written into the context as actual tokens, and the next forward pass runs the entire stack again with that result available. Chain-of-thought converts a depth limit into a length limit. Each generated token buys you another complete pass through every layer, with the previous conclusion now in the input.

That's why it helps enormously on multi-step arithmetic, logic and planning — problems with genuine sequential dependencies — and does nothing for sentiment classification or format conversion, which need one step. And it's why forcing a one-word answer to a hard question isn't efficiency. It's removing the only mechanism the model has for going deeper than its own layer count.

Three reasons, and the third is the one that catches people.

No steps to take. If the task needs one step, extra steps compute nothing. You've bought a longer bill.

15Read "lost in the middle" off the architecture

A well-replicated finding: put a fact at the start of a long context and the model uses it; put it at the end and the model uses it; put it in the middle and performance drops noticeably. It looks arbitrary. From the architecture it isn't.

Three effects stack up. The attention sink pulls large amounts of weight to the earliest tokens for structural reasons, so early positions get read reliably. Recency favours the end — position encodings and the shorter distance make nearby tokens easier to attend to. And the softmax budget means everything in between is competing against a very large number of similar-looking neighbours, none of which stands out.

So the shape is a U-curve, and it is a property of the mechanism rather than a training defect. Newer models mitigate it; none has eliminated it.

Two rules follow. Put what matters at the ends. Framing at the top, the critical instruction and the question at the bottom, the bulk in the middle. And if a specific fact is essential, don't rely on the model finding it in the middle of a haystack — retrieve it and place it deliberately.

16See the system prompt for what it is: tokens in a costume

Everything in this module converges here, and it's the point where architecture becomes a security position.

A system prompt feels like configuration. You set it once, it's separate from user input in the API, and it governs behaviour. That framing implies privilege. There is none.

Mechanically, the SDK takes your system prompt, wraps it in special marker tokens from the chat template, and puts it at the front of the same single sequence as everything else. Those markers are tokens the model was trained to weight heavily. That's a strong statistical habit, not a permission boundary. Instruction-hierarchy training has made models much better at preferring system instructions over conflicting user content — genuinely better, measurably so — but "trained to prefer" is a different category of thing from "unable to do otherwise."

This is why prompt injection is structural and not a bug awaiting a patch. There's no parser boundary because there's no second channel. Instructions and data are the same kind of object, in the same stream, processed by the same layers. You cannot escape data into a place where it stops being read as language, because being read as language is the product.

So the defensive posture is the one from the first module, now with a mechanical justification: assume the model can be talked out of any instruction, and design so that being talked out of it is survivable. The boundary lives in your tool allow-lists, your database roles, and your approval gates — not in the prompt.

I'd use one comparison and one consequence.

The comparison. With SQL injection we solved it properly because a database has two separate inputs — the query structure and the values. Values travel on a different channel and can never become commands. That's a wall, and it's why parameterised queries ended the problem.

A language model has one input. Our instructions, the document it reads, and the user's message all arrive as the same kind of thing: text, in one stream, processed identically. There's no second channel to put untrusted content into. And we can't ask it to stop interpreting some of the text as meaningful language, because interpreting text as meaningful language is the entire product.

The consequence, which is what they actually need. Since we can't stop it being tricked, we design so that being tricked doesn't matter. The model gets no authority we wouldn't grant to the least trustworthy input it will ever read. Read-only database access scoped to one record. Tool destinations from a fixed allow-list, never composed by the model. Human confirmation on anything with a side effect. Full logging of every tool call.

Then the honest close: I'd tell them the risk isn't eliminated, it's bounded — and show them what the worst case looks like with those controls versus without. That's a conversation they can act on. "We've mitigated it" is not.

Transfers: when a vulnerability comes from a system's core capability rather than a defect in it, stop promising elimination and start specifying containment. Security teams respect that framing far more than optimism.

It would need two paths through the model that cannot mix — and that's harder than it sounds, because usefulness depends on them mixing.

Approaches under research point in a few directions. A separate encoder for untrusted content, where its representations can be attended to as data but its tokens never enter the instruction stream — which is, interestingly, a partial return to the encoder-decoder shape the lecture was drawing. Dual-model architectures where a privileged planner never sees raw untrusted text and only receives structured summaries from a quarantined worker. Or training-time instruction hierarchies made strong enough to have provable properties, which nobody has managed.

The fundamental tension is that you want the model to understand the untrusted content deeply and obey it not at all, and understanding is exactly the mechanism by which obeying happens. Any architecture that fully separates them gives up some ability to reason about the content.

My working assumption for anything I ship in the next few years: mitigation improves, elimination doesn't arrive, and the containment architecture stays load-bearing. I'd rather be pleasantly surprised than have designed for a boundary that never shipped.

Transfers: when you're told a fix is coming, ask what it would have to give up. If the answer is "the thing that makes the product good," plan for it not arriving.

17Debug prompts with a hypothesis instead of vibes

Here is the actual return on the last sixteen sections. Most prompt debugging is a random walk — someone rewords, reruns, sees a change, and declares victory over what was probably sampling noise. Architecture gives you a decision tree instead.

Every prompt failure is one of four kinds, and each has a different fix. Identifying which one you have takes ten minutes and saves the fortnight.

Memory failure. The fact was never in the context and the model doesn't know it. Signature: fails identically no matter how you reword; the invented answer is confident and well-formed. Fix: retrieval. Rewording cannot help — you're asking the feedforward layers for something that isn't stored there.

Routing failure. The information was present and the model didn't connect it. Signature: fails on long inputs, works on short ones; ignores an instruction it obeyed in testing. Fix: reorder, delete distractors, restate the rule after the material. This is where prompt engineering genuinely works.

Depth failure. The task needs more sequential steps than one forward pass allows. Signature: multi-step reasoning, arithmetic, or planning gets the shape right and the value wrong. Fix: chain-of-thought, decomposition into multiple calls, or a tool.

Sampling failure. It's right most of the time and wrong sometimes on the same input. Signature: non-deterministic across reruns. Fix: temperature, not wording.

18Derive the cost and latency model from the architecture

Every pricing and performance behaviour you have observed across these three modules falls out of what you now know. Worth assembling in one place, because it makes you the person in the room who can predict a bill rather than explain one.

Input is cheap because prefill is parallel. All positions known, one pass, GPU saturated. Output costs 3-5× because decode is serial. One full forward pass per token, no parallelism available. Cached prefixes cost ~10% because the mask freezes them. The vendor genuinely skips the work.

Long conversations get slower, not just pricier, because the KV cache must be re-read every decode step and you become memory-bandwidth-bound. Concurrency is capped by KV memory, not by compute — which is why providers limit sessions and why self-hosted capacity is measured in concurrent context.

Reasoning tiers cost disproportionately because the scratchpad is output tokens, the expensive kind, and you don't see them. Indic languages compound because the token multiplier hits everything linear and the attention term quadratically.

None of that is pricing policy. All of it is a consequence of a triangle, a softmax, and a serial decoder.

Speculative decoding is the technique that's actually shipped. A small fast model drafts several tokens ahead; the large model verifies them all in one parallel pass and accepts the prefix that matches what it would have produced. When the draft is good you get several tokens for one big-model pass. Crucially the output is identical to what the big model would have produced alone — it's pure speedup, no quality trade. This is why decoding got noticeably faster across the industry without models changing.

Diffusion language models are the more radical bet: generate all positions at once and refine iteratively, the way image models do. That would break the serial constraint outright. Early results exist; quality at scale hasn't matched autoregressive models yet.

My working assumption: the serial property softens through better speculation and batching, but stays the fundamental shape for the near term. So designs that assume output tokens are expensive will keep being right, and designs betting on output becoming cheap are betting on a research outcome.

Transfers: when a bottleneck comes from a definition rather than an implementation, the workarounds are usually "do it speculatively and verify" — the same pattern as branch prediction and optimistic concurrency.

"""
Multi-head causal self-attention, from scratch. pip install numpy

This is the real operation, not a simplification. Every claim in
this module is visible in the output: the triangle, the budget
summing to 1, the KV cache, and heads doing different jobs.
"""
import numpy as np
np.random.seed(0)
np.set_printoptions(precision=2, suppress=True)

def softmax(x, axis=-1):
    # Subtract the max for numerical stability. Standard trick:
    # exp() of a large number overflows; shifting doesn't change
    # the result because softmax is invariant to a constant shift.
    x = x - x.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)
    e = np.exp(x)
    return e / e.sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True)

def attention(Q, K, V, causal=True):
    """Q,K,V: (n_tokens, d_head). Returns (output, weights)."""
    n, d = Q.shape

    # 1. SCORES: every query dotted against every key.
    #    This n x n matrix IS the quadratic cost (section 08).
    scores = Q @ K.T                       # (n, n)

    # 2. SCALE by sqrt(d). Without this, dot products of long
    #    vectors get large, softmax saturates, gradients vanish.
    #    This one line is why the paper says "scaled" dot-product.
    scores = scores / np.sqrt(d)

    # 3. THE CAUSAL MASK (section 03). Everything above the
    #    diagonal becomes -inf, so softmax makes it exactly 0.
    #    This single line is why: representations freeze, the KV
    #    cache works, prompt caching works, and there is no undo.
    if causal:
        mask = np.triu(np.ones((n, n), dtype=bool), k=1)
        scores = np.where(mask, -np.inf, scores)

    # 4. SOFTMAX -> a probability distribution per row.
    #    Each row sums to EXACTLY 1.0. That is the fixed budget:
    #    attention on one token is attention taken from another.
    W = softmax(scores)                    # (n, n)

    # 5. WEIGHTED SUM of values. Retrieval, but soft.
    return W @ V, W

def multi_head(X, n_heads=4):
    """X: (n_tokens, d_model). Heads run in parallel, then concat."""
    n, d_model = X.shape
    d_head = d_model // n_heads
    outs, all_w = [], []
    for h in range(n_heads):
        # Each head has its OWN learned projections - which is why
        # heads specialise (section 07). Random here; trained in reality.
        Wq = np.random.randn(d_model, d_head) * 0.1
        Wk = np.random.randn(d_model, d_head) * 0.1
        Wv = np.random.randn(d_model, d_head) * 0.1
        o, w = attention(X @ Wq, X @ Wk, X @ Wv)
        outs.append(o); all_w.append(w)
    return np.concatenate(outs, axis=-1), all_w

CODE · PYTHONdiagnose.py — the §17 decision tree as a harness
"""
Runs the four-way diagnostic from section 17 against a real prompt
so you stop guessing which failure you have.
pip install "anthropic>=0.40"
"""
import os, json, statistics
from anthropic import Anthropic

client = Anthropic()
MODEL = "claude-sonnet-4-5-20250929"      # pinned, never an alias

def ask(system, user, temperature=0.0):
    r = client.messages.create(model=MODEL, max_tokens=600,
                               temperature=temperature, system=system,
                               messages=[{"role":"user","content":user}])
    return r.content[0].text.strip()

def diagnose(system, user, expected, context_text=""):
    """Four checks, in the order that eliminates the most work first."""
    report = {}

    # CHECK 1 - SAMPLING. Same input, 5 runs, production temperature.
    # If answers differ, you have a decoding problem, not a prompt
    # problem. This is the cheapest check and it is almost never run.
    runs = [ask(system, user, temperature=0.7) for _ in range(5)]
    report["unique_answers_at_temp_0.7"] = len(set(runs))
    if len(set(runs)) > 1:
        report["verdict"] = "SAMPLING - set temperature to 0 before anything else"
        return report

    # CHECK 2 - MEMORY. Was the needed information physically present?
    # Do this by inspection, not by asking the model - it will happily
    # tell you the fact was there when it was not.
    fact_present = expected.lower() in context_text.lower()
    report["fact_in_context"] = fact_present
    if not fact_present:
        report["verdict"] = "MEMORY - add retrieval. rewording cannot fix this"
        return report

    # CHECK 3 - ROUTING. Does it pass on a short context and fail on
    # a long one? That is attention dilution, and it is fixable by
    # placement: restate the rule AFTER the document (section 12).
    short_ok = expected.lower() in ask(system, context_text[:1500] + "\n" + user).lower()
    long_ok  = expected.lower() in ask(system, context_text + "\n" + user).lower()
    report["passes_short"], report["passes_long"] = short_ok, long_ok
    if short_ok and not long_ok:
        report["verdict"] = "ROUTING - restate the rule after the document; cut distractors"
        return report

    # CHECK 4 - DEPTH. Give it room to work. If forcing a scratchpad
    # fixes it, the task needed more sequential steps than one
    # forward pass allows (section 14).
    cot = ask(system + "\nWork through this step by step before answering.",
              context_text + "\n" + user)
    report["passes_with_reasoning"] = expected.lower() in cot.lower()
    report["verdict"] = ("DEPTH - use chain-of-thought, decompose, or give it a tool"
                         if report["passes_with_reasoning"]
                         else "NONE OF THE FOUR - suspect an ambiguous task or a "
                              "label error. check inter-annotator agreement.")
    return report

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(json.dumps(diagnose(
        system="You answer only from the DOCUMENT. If absent, say NOT_FOUND.",
        user="What is the foreclosure charge on the auto loan?",
        expected="3%",
        context_text=open("policy.txt").read(),
    ), indent=2))

Glossary

Every term used above, one plain sentence each.

ALiBi
An alternative to RoPE that encodes position by penalising attention with distance rather than rotating vectors.
Attention
An operation where each token retrieves a weighted blend of information from other tokens, with the weights decided by learned content matching.
Attention sink
The first token or two absorbing large amounts of attention weight for structural reasons, because the softmax must allocate a total of 1.
Autoregression
Generating each token conditioned on all previous ones, which is why the model can never revise.
BERT
An encoder-only model family that reads bidirectionally and cannot generate text; still widely used for embeddings and classification.
Causal mask
The rule forbidding a token from attending to later positions, implemented by setting those scores to negative infinity before the softmax.
Chain-of-thought
Prompting the model to generate reasoning before its answer, which buys extra forward passes and writes intermediate results into the context.
Chat template
The special marker tokens an SDK inserts around system, user and assistant turns to signal role structure.
Cross-attention
Attention from a decoder to an encoder's output; present in translation models and absent from GPT-style models.
d_model
The width of the residual stream — how many numbers represent each token at each layer.
Decoder-only
The architecture of modern chat LLMs: one stack, one sequence, causal masking throughout, no encoder.
Decode
The generation phase, producing one token per forward pass; serial and the expensive half of the bill.
Depth
The number of layers, which sets how many dependent reasoning steps fit in one forward pass.
Encoder-decoder
The original 2017 architecture with two stacks, built for tasks with a distinct source and target such as translation.
Feedforward network
The per-position block holding roughly two thirds of a layer's parameters, best understood as the model's stored knowledge.
FlashAttention
An implementation that keeps the attention computation in fast on-chip memory, cutting data movement without changing the maths.
Grouped-query attention
Sharing key and value heads across several query heads, shrinking the KV cache by the sharing ratio at little quality cost.
Head
One independent attention operation within a layer, with its own query, key and value projections.
In-context learning
The misleading name for few-shot prompting; no weights change, the model is completing a pattern it can see.
Induction head
A two-step attention circuit that finds where the current pattern occurred earlier and copies what followed it.
Key
The vector a token advertises so other tokens can decide whether it is relevant to them.
KV cache
Stored key and value vectors for earlier tokens, reusable because the causal mask makes them immutable.
Layer normalisation
Rescaling activations to keep training stable; applied after each block in 2017 and before each block in modern models.
Logits
The raw scores over the whole vocabulary produced by the final linear layer, before softmax.
Lost in the middle
The observed U-shaped drop in reliability for information placed in the middle of a long context.
Mixture of experts
Replacing one feedforward block with many, activating only a few per token, so total and active parameter counts differ.
Multi-head attention
Several attention operations running in parallel in one layer, each free to track a different relationship.
Multi-query attention
The extreme of grouped-query attention, with a single shared key/value head.
Positional encoding
Information about token order injected deliberately, because attention itself is order-blind.
Post-norm / pre-norm
Whether normalisation is applied after or before each block; modern models use pre-norm because it trains more stably at depth.
Prefill
The phase where the whole prompt is processed in one parallel pass; fast and the cheap half of the bill.
Prompt caching
A vendor feature billing a repeated prefix at a large discount, made possible by the causal mask freezing earlier states.
Query
The vector describing what a token is looking for in the rest of the sequence.
Residual stream
The running total at each position that every block reads from and adds to; the model's working memory through depth.
RoPE
Rotary position embedding — encoding position by rotating query and key vectors, so attention depends on relative distance.
Self-attention
Attention within one sequence, as opposed to attention from one sequence to another.
Softmax
The function turning raw scores into a distribution summing to exactly one — which is why attention is a fixed budget.
Speculative decoding
Using a small model to draft several tokens that the large model verifies in one pass, speeding generation without changing output.
Sliding-window attention
Restricting each token to attend only to a nearby window, trading reach for cost at long context.
Transformer
The 2017 architecture built entirely on attention, which replaced recurrence and made parallel training possible.
Value
The information a token contributes when other tokens attend to it.
YaRN
One of several methods for extending a model's usable context beyond its trained length by rescaling position encodings.
