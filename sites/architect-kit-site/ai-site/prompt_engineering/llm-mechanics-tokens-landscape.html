PART I

The mechanism, one token at a time

−

The Ottawa example is the best thing in this section of the course. It is also simplified in three specific ways, and each simplification hides something you need. This part unpacks all three.

01Watch one token actually get chosen

The lecture's picture: you write "The capital of Canada is", the model considers Ottawa at 70%, Toronto at 15%, Vancouver at a bit less, and picks Ottawa. That is the right shape and the wrong scale.

The model does not choose between four Canadian cities. At every single step it produces a score for every token in its vocabulary — typically 100,000 to 200,000 of them. Those scores are called logits. A softmax function turns them into probabilities that add up to 1. Then one gets picked.

So the real distribution includes Ottawa, yes. It also includes " the", " a", " located", " home", " known", " situated", a comma, a newline, the Korean word for water, and 199,000 other things. Most of the probability mass sits on tokens that are not cities at all — because "The capital of Canada is the city of Ottawa" and "The capital of Canada is located on the Ottawa River" are both perfectly ordinary continuations.

That scale matters for one practical reason. When people say "the model was 70% confident," they usually imagine it weighing a handful of candidate answers. It is not. It is ranking a dictionary. And the ranking is over what text comes next, not over what is true.

Sketch 1 · Why "70% confident" is not the sentence you think it is
Trap · Probability is not confidence in truth

The model is not 70% sure that Ottawa is the capital of Canada. It is 70% sure that " Ottawa" is the text that comes next. Those are different claims. Ask it about a circular that does not exist and it will assign high probability to a plausible-looking number, because once the sentence has started, that number is exactly what should come next. High probability, zero truth.

Because the same answer is spread across multiple tokens. " Ottawa", " Ottawa,", " Ott" and "Ottawa" without the leading space are four distinct entries in the vocabulary. If you read the top token's probability as "confidence in the answer", you are systematically underestimating it — the real confidence in the concept is the sum across all the token paths that lead there.

This is why naive logprob-based confidence gating is fiddly. The clean version is to constrain output so there's only one legal way to express each answer — an enum, a schema, a single-token label like A/B/C. Then one token carries the whole probability.

Rule: if you plan to read confidence from token probabilities, first make sure each answer has exactly one spelling.

Because "just" is doing enormous work in that sentence. Predicting the next token well, across the entire internet, is not an easy objective that happens to produce fluent text. To predict the next token in a chess transcript you need something like a board state. To predict it in a Python traceback you need something like a model of the program. To predict the last word of a murder mystery you need to have tracked who was where.

So the training objective is simple and the internal machinery it forces into existence is not. Dismissing it as autocomplete predicts the wrong failures — it makes you expect it to be bad at reasoning, when actually it's often good at reasoning and bad at counting characters, which autocomplete-thinking would never have predicted.

The useful framing is neither "it thinks" nor "it's just autocomplete". It's: the model has learned whatever internal structure was needed to predict text, and nothing else. Tasks that were well-represented in text, it does well. Tasks that text never required — arithmetic on long numbers, character counting, knowing what it doesn't know — it does badly, no matter how simple they look to you.

Transfers: when you hear a dismissive one-liner about a system ("it's just a lookup table", "it's just regex"), ask what the objective forced it to build internally. That's what predicts behaviour, not the label.

02Correct the lecture: "it picks the highest" is only one strategy

The lecture says the model "selects the highest probability" and gives you Ottawa. That is greedy decoding, and it is not what ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini do by default.

By default they sample. The distribution is drawn from, not maximised over. That is why you can ask the same question twice and get two different answers — a fact the lecture states nowhere, even though every student will observe it within an hour.

Four strategies are worth knowing by name. Greedy always takes the top token; repeatable, and prone to dull, looping text. Temperature reshapes the distribution before sampling — low sharpens it toward greedy, high flattens it toward chaos. Top-k keeps only the k highest tokens then samples among them. Top-p (nucleus) keeps the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities sum to p, which adapts: when the model is certain, that's one token; when it's unsure, it's fifty.

The practical point is that the same model with the same prompt is a different product at different settings. If your evaluation ran at temperature 0 and production runs at 0.7, you have not tested production.

Sketch 2 · The knob the lecture leaves at whatever the vendor set
Warn · The setting the course never mentions

If your prompt "sometimes works," check the temperature before you rewrite a single word. Half the prompt-tuning effort spent in the industry is people fighting sampling noise with adjectives. Set temperature to 0, re-run, and see whether the problem is the prompt or the dice.

Three questions, in order, because they cost minutes and can eliminate the whole investigation.

What's the temperature? If it's the vendor default — often 0.7 or 1.0 — a classification or extraction task is being asked to roll dice. Setting it to 0 may take the 10% to 2% before anyone edits a prompt.

Is it the same 1 in 10, or a random 1 in 10? Run the same 100 inputs three times. If the failures are the same inputs each time, it's a capability or prompt problem and worth real work. If the failures are different inputs each time, it's sampling variance and the fix is decoding parameters, not wording. Nobody does this test and it separates two completely different projects.

Is 90% actually failing? Some tasks have a human ceiling below 95%. If two of our own annotators disagree 12% of the time, chasing 99% is chasing noise.

Only after those three would I look at the prompt. And I'd want the failures bucketed by type first — see the error taxonomy discipline from Section 01.

Transfers: before debugging logic, check whether the system is deterministic. Half of "flaky" in any domain is a config default nobody set.

Because the most likely token at each step is not the most likely sentence. Greedy is locally optimal and globally short-sighted. Taking a 62% token now can lead into a region where every continuation is poor, while taking a 20% token now opens onto a much better sentence. Greedy can never discover that, because it never looks ahead and it cannot revise.

There's a second, weirder problem: greedy text is too predictable to read well. Human writing is full of moderately surprising word choices. Text that maximises likelihood at every step comes out flat, repetitive, and prone to loops — "the the the" style degeneration is a real observed failure. Some randomness makes output more human-like, not less.

So the honest picture is a trade. Greedy buys repeatability and costs quality and liveliness. Sampling buys quality and costs repeatability. Which you want depends entirely on whether the output is a decision or a piece of prose — which is why the answer is per-task, not global.

Transfers: greedy algorithms fail the same way everywhere — locally optimal, globally poor. The fix in search is lookahead; here it's sampling plus, at the top end, letting the model reason before it commits.

03The buried lede: there is no backspace

The line

"It predicts word by word, token by token... each token is selected based on the probabilities from the training data."

The lecturer says it four or five times. He never says the consequence, and the consequence is the most important mechanical fact about these systems.

Each token is generated with all previous tokens as input. That includes the tokens the model itself just produced. This is called autoregression, and it has a property nobody states plainly: the model cannot revise.

There is no backspace. There is no draft. Once a token is emitted, it is in the context, and every subsequent token is conditioned on it as if it were true. The model does not know it made a mistake, and even if it somehow did, it has no mechanism to go back and change it. All it can do is continue coherently from where it now is.

Now watch what that produces. The model starts an answer with a wrong first token — say it begins "Yes," to a question where the answer is no. Every following token is now being chosen to make a coherent continuation of "Yes,". The model will construct a justification. It is not lying. It is doing the only thing it can do: producing the most likely continuation of a sentence that has already gone wrong.

This is why hallucinations do not stay small. One invented circular number becomes a paragraph of invented provisions, because a paragraph explaining a circular is what most plausibly follows a circular number. The error does not sit there. It recruits.

Sketch 3 · Why a small error never stays small

Three practical consequences follow directly, and they are worth memorising because they look like unrelated tips until you see the mechanism.

One: force the reasoning before the answer, never after. If your output format puts the verdict first and the explanation second, the explanation is a rationalisation of a verdict the model already committed to. Put the reasoning field first in the schema and the verdict last. Same tokens, completely different quality.

Two: a short answer to a hard question is a guess. Asking for one word forces the model to commit at token one, with no room to work. That is not efficiency. That is removing the only mechanism it has.

Three: retrying a wrong answer is cheaper than arguing with it. Once bad tokens are in the context, they keep influencing everything downstream — including the model's own corrections. Regenerate from a clean context rather than saying "no, try again" in the same conversation.

I'd say the field order in the schema isn't a presentation choice, it's a computation order, and putting the decision first will measurably degrade quality.

The model generates left to right and cannot revise. If {"decision": ...} comes first, the model commits to the verdict before it has generated a single token of analysis. Everything in the reasoning field afterwards is then constructed to justify a decision already made. You get a confident verdict with a fluent post-hoc rationalisation, and the rationalisation will look convincing precisely because it was written to fit.

Put reasoning first and decision last, and the model has generated the relevant considerations into its own context before it commits. That's not a style preference — it's giving the computation somewhere to happen.

The fix for the reviewer's actual concern is trivial: reorder the fields in the UI, after parsing. The wire format and the display format don't have to match. I'd offer that immediately so the conversation doesn't become adversarial.

Transfers: whenever a system computes in a fixed order, the schema is a program, not a document. Same instinct as knowing that column order in a composite index is not cosmetic.

They don't revise. They generate more text, and some of that text happens to be self-correction.

The trick is that a reasoning model is trained to produce a long internal scratchpad before its final answer. Inside that scratchpad it can write "wait, that gives 47, but the constraint said under 40 — let me redo this." That correction is just more tokens, appended like any other. Nothing was erased. But because the final answer is generated after the correction, the final answer is conditioned on the correction rather than on the mistake.

So the mechanism is: give the model enough room to make the mistake, notice it in text, and then answer. Revision is simulated by append-only self-commentary. It works surprisingly well and it explains the cost profile — reasoning models can burn thousands of hidden output tokens per request, and output tokens are the expensive kind.

It also explains a failure mode. If the scratchpad goes wrong early and confidently, the model can reason itself deeper into a wrong answer with impressive rigour. More thinking is not monotonically better.

Transfers: in an append-only system, "undo" is always implemented as "write a correction and make sure the reader sees it last." True of event sourcing, ledgers, and this.

Because the wrong answer is still in the context, and everything the model now generates is conditioned on it. You have not deleted the error; you have added a note next to it. The model frequently produces a variation on the same wrong answer, or agrees with you and invents a different wrong answer to satisfy the correction.

Starting a fresh call with a better prompt gives the model a clean context. In production, this is the difference between a conversational retry loop and a stateless regenerate — and the stateless one is both cheaper and more accurate.

Rule: correct the prompt, not the model. Arguing with a transcript only makes the transcript longer.

04Read chain-of-thought as extra compute, not a magic phrase

Everyone has heard "let's think step by step." Most people treat it as a spell. It isn't. Once you have section 03, it's obvious what it does.

The model does a fixed amount of computation per token. Not per problem — per token. A hard question and an easy question get the same number of forward passes if they get the same number of output tokens. So if you demand a one-word answer to a multi-step problem, you have given the model one token's worth of computation to solve it. It will not solve it. It will produce the most plausible-looking one-word answer.

Asking for reasoning does two things at once. It gives the model more forward passes — more computation, literally. And it puts intermediate results into the context, so later steps can condition on earlier ones instead of holding everything implicitly. The scratchpad is external working memory.

That reframe tells you when it helps and when it's waste. Multi-step arithmetic, logic, planning, anything with dependencies: big gains. Sentiment classification, format conversion, lookup: no gain, just cost. And on modern reasoning models it can be actively redundant, because they already generate a hidden scratchpad — you're paying twice for the same mechanism.

You don't always need a full scratchpad. Just ordering your output schema so the analysis fields come before the verdict field gets most of the benefit at a fraction of the tokens, and it stays machine-parseable. {"evidence": "...", "risk_factors": [...], "decision": "..."} is a chain-of-thought prompt wearing a schema.

Transfers: blanket changes across a codebase are always a measurement question. "It helped on the one I tried" is a sample size of one.

05Accept that the model has never seen a letter

The lecture gets to the important sentence and then walks past it: "each token is a numeric ID under the hood that the model processes."

Take that literally, because it is literal. The text you type is converted to integers before the model touches it. " Ottawa" becomes something like 68276. The model sees 68276. It never sees O-t-t-a-w-a. It has no access to the spelling of its own vocabulary.

It knows a great deal about spelling — because text discussing spelling was in the training data — the way you know things about a city you have never visited. But it cannot look. When you ask how many r's are in "strawberry", you are asking someone to count features of an object they have only ever read descriptions of.

This single fact predicts a whole family of failures that otherwise look random and embarrassing.

Note · Why this keeps surprising people

The failures cluster on tasks that look trivially easy to a human, which makes the model look unreliable in general. It isn't — it's unreliable on a specific, predictable class. Once you know the class, you route around it. Anything character-level, positional, or exact-count goes to code. Everything else can go to the model.

No — and how they got better is the interesting part.

Three things happened, none of which is "the model can now see letters". Memorisation: the strawberry example became famous, so text discussing it entered the training data and the model learned the answer as a fact. Reasoning: models trained to use a scratchpad will spell the word out token by token first — "s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y" — which converts an invisible property into visible tokens it can then count. Tools: many products now quietly run Python for anything countable.

The limitation is intact. Test it with a word nobody has blogged about and no scratchpad allowed, and it returns. That's the general lesson: when a famous failure disappears, check whether the mechanism changed or just that specific instance got patched by fame.

Transfers: benchmark contamination works the same way. A model acing a famous test tells you less every year the test stays famous.

Both go to code, and I'd push back hard on the second one especially.

PAN format validation is five letters, four digits, one letter. That's a regex. It's exact, free, instant, testable, and it cannot hallucinate a pass. Asking a model to do it means accepting a small error rate on something with a zero-error solution.

Card masking is worse than inefficient, it's a security defect. It's a character-position operation, which is precisely the class the model is unreliable at — it may mask the wrong digits or drop one. And more importantly, to mask a card number you must send the full card number to a third party. We've created a PCI exposure to do a substring operation. That's not a trade-off, it's just wrong.

Where I would use the model on this journey: pulling a PAN out of a messy free-text message where the customer wrote it with spaces, in a sentence, possibly with a typo. That's fuzzy extraction and it's genuinely hard for regex. Extract with the model, then validate with code, then mask with code. Model for the fuzzy part, code for the exact part — that split is the whole design.

Transfers: "can the model do it?" is the wrong question. "Is there a deterministic solution?" comes first. If yes, the model is a more expensive, less reliable version of something you already have.
PART II

Tokens, properly

06Re-run the lecture's examples and read them properly

The demo produced four observations. All four are correct. Each one has a consequence the lecture doesn't draw.

"The capital of Canada is Ottawa" is six tokens. Six tokens, thirty-one characters. That's the ~4 characters-per-token ratio for ordinary English that every cost estimate in the industry rests on. Note it only holds for ordinary English.

The space belongs to the following word. " capital" with its leading space is one token; "capital" without is a different token entirely. This is why prompt formatting is slightly load-bearing — a trailing space at the end of your prompt can change which token comes next and measurably degrade output. It's a real, documented gotcha.

"Unbelievable" splits into pieces. Common words get one token; rarer words get assembled from fragments. That's the whole design: a fixed vocabulary that can still represent any string, by falling back to smaller and smaller pieces. It also means rare words cost more — including your product names, Indian city names, and technical jargon.

Apostrophes and punctuation are separate tokens. "Let's" is "Let" + "'s". An exclamation mark is its own token. Punctuation is not free.

Two reasons, and the second is the one that killed the idea.

Vocabulary size. English alone has hundreds of thousands of word forms; add names, typos, code identifiers, and every other language, and you need millions of entries. Out-of-vocabulary words. This is the fatal one. A word-level model meeting a word it has never seen has nothing to do — no representation exists. 07Know the four things tokenization silently breaks

The lecture says tokens affect "model behavior, performance and cost." True, and stated so briefly it sounds like a footnote. Here are the four places it actually bites, all of which will reach you in a fintech context.

Numbers. Digits do not chunk consistently. Depending on the tokenizer, 480000 might be one token, or 48+0000, or 4+800+00. The model has to do arithmetic on chunks whose boundaries don't align with place value. This is a large part of why LLM arithmetic on long numbers is unreliable, and it gets worse with the Indian grouping convention — 4,80,000 tokenizes differently from 480,000, and both differently from 480000.

Indic scripts. Covered in the previous module and worth repeating: Tamil, Telugu, Bengali and Devanagari fragment into far more tokens than English for the same meaning. Three to six times is typical. That is cost, latency, and usable context window all at once.

Structured formats. JSON, XML and code are punctuation-dense, and punctuation is tokens. Indentation is tokens. A deeply nested JSON schema in your system prompt is genuinely expensive. It's usually still the right call — the reliability is worth it — but flatten your schemas and don't pretty-print what the model doesn't need to read.

Anything with an exact format. Account numbers, IFSC codes, UPI IDs, PAN, GSTIN. The model sees these as arbitrary fragments and will happily produce a well-shaped, invalid one. Never let a model generate an identifier. Extract, look up, validate — but not generate.

08Treat the tokenizer as a versioned dependency

The lecture shows a model dropdown in the tokenizer tool and moves past it. That dropdown is the point. Different models use different tokenizers, and the same text produces different token counts on each.

OpenAI has shipped several generations — cl100k_base for the GPT-4 era, o200k_base for later models with a larger vocabulary and better multilingual coverage. Anthropic, Google, Meta and Mistral each use their own. A prompt that costs 1,000 tokens on one may cost 1,300 on another, and the gap widens sharply for non-English text and code.

Three consequences follow, and all three surprise teams.

Your cost model is model-specific. Comparing two vendors on price-per-million-tokens is incomplete; you also need tokens-per-request on your actual text. A model with a 20% higher list price can be cheaper in practice if its tokenizer is 30% more efficient on Tamil. Compare cost per request, never cost per token.

Your context budget is model-specific. The same document fits in one model's window and overflows another's, even at identical stated window sizes.

Token counts change when the vendor changes tokenizer. A model upgrade can shift your bill and your truncation behaviour with no code change on your side.

A tokenizer's vocabulary is a fixed budget — say 200,000 slots — allocated by frequency in the training corpus. Every slot given to a common Tamil word is a slot not given to something else. Transfers: shared fixed-size resources allocated by aggregate frequency always underserve minorities within the aggregate. True of tokenizers, cache lines, and default form fields.

10Stop saying "open source" — say open weights, and read the licence

This is the most consequential correction in the whole module, so it gets stated plainly. Llama is not open source. It is open weights. The lecturer says "fully open source" twice, and it is not a small slip.

Llama ships under Meta's own Llama Community License, not Apache 2.0 or MIT. That licence carries real conditions: an acceptable use policy restricting what you may build, a requirement that products display "Built with Llama," a rule that derivative models must begin their name with "Llama," a redistribution obligation to carry Meta's licence text, and a threshold above which very large platforms need a separate grant from Meta. Some Llama releases have also carried regional restrictions.

The Open Source Initiative — the body that defines the term — has said directly that Llama's licence does not meet the Open Source Definition, because it discriminates between users and between fields of use. That's not a philosophical quibble. It's the difference between a term your legal team recognises and one they don't.

Mistral is a similar but different story. Some Mistral models genuinely are Apache 2.0. Others ship under a research-only licence or are commercial-only. "Mistral is open" is not a true sentence about a company. It is only ever a true sentence about a specific model release. Same for Google's Gemma, Alibaba's Qwen, DeepSeek — each family has its own terms, and they change between releases.

11Separate the model, the product, and the endpoint

Hold three layers apart, because they have different names, different prices, different terms, and different compliance profiles.

The model is the weights. It has a version and a snapshot date. This is what determines quality. The product is the consumer app — the web chat, the mobile app. It has a subscription price, a system prompt you don't control, memory features, safety layers, and its own terms of service. The endpoint is how your code reaches the model — a REST API, a cloud region, a VPC. It has per-token pricing, a data-handling contract, and a jurisdiction.

12Pick a deployment mode before you pick a model

The lecture's access column has two values: "own website" and "API," plus "you cannot run it locally." That's a two-option world. There are four, and the choice between them decides more about your project than the model does.

Mode 1 — vendor API direct. You call the model vendor. Simplest, fastest to start, newest models first. Your data crosses into their infrastructure under their terms, usually in their chosen region.

Mode 2 — cloud marketplace. AWS Bedrock, Google Vertex AI, Azure AI Foundry. You call a third-party model, but the call terminates inside your cloud account, in your chosen region, under your existing cloud contract. This is the option the lecture never mentions and it is the one most Indian regulated entities actually use.

Mode 3 — self-hosted open weights. You download weights and serve them on your own GPUs, in your VPC or your data centre. Maximum control, maximum responsibility.

Mode 4 — on-device. Small models running on a phone or laptop. Data never leaves the device. Quality is limited but for narrow tasks it can be enough, and the privacy story is unbeatable.

Note what mode 2 does to the lecturer's claim that closed models "cannot run locally." Strictly true — but you can often run them in your own account, in an Indian region, without your data reaching the model vendor at all. For compliance purposes that difference is the whole ballgame.

13Read the aggregator layer for what it is

14Route across a tier ladder instead of marrying one model

The lecture's implicit question is "which model should I use?" It's the wrong question, and every vendor's own product structure tells you so.

Notice that each family ships a tier ladder: a small fast cheap model, a mid balanced one, a large expensive one, and increasingly a reasoning tier that thinks before answering. Vendors use different names — nano/mini/pro, haiku/sonnet/opus, flash/pro — but the ladder is the same everywhere, because the underlying economics are the same everywhere.

They build ladders because real traffic is not uniformly hard. Most requests in most workloads are easy. Sending them all to your best model means paying premium rates for the easy majority and adding latency users can feel.

So the useful question is not "which model" but "which model for which request." Classify difficulty cheaply, route accordingly, escalate on low confidence. In practice a deterministic layer catches a chunk, a small model handles the bulk, and only a fraction reaches the frontier tier.

Warn · Reasoning tiers are not a free upgrade

Reasoning models generate a long hidden scratchpad before answering. Those are output tokens — the expensive kind — and they can be several times the visible answer. On a high-volume, low-difficulty path a reasoning model can cost an order of magnitude more for no measurable gain. Route to it deliberately; never make it the default because it scored highest on a benchmark.

Because the routing layer needs something most teams don't have, and building it feels like a detour from shipping.

To route you need a confidence signal — some way to know the cheap model got it right. Small models don't hand you a calibrated one. So you have to build a proxy: a self-rated confidence field, agreement between two cheap runs, a schema-completeness check, or a classifier trained on your own escalation history. Each is a small project, and none is worth doing before you have an eval suite to tell you whether the cheap path is actually equivalent.

There's also an organisational reason. Cost optimisation has no advocate early on. The bill is small during pilot, the team is measured on shipping, and "make it 80% cheaper" doesn't get prioritised until finance notices. By then the single-model architecture is load-bearing and retrofitting a router touches everything.

Which is the argument for doing it early — not building the full cascade on day one, but putting every call behind one internal client so the routing decision has somewhere to live later. That's an afternoon of work at the start and a quarter of work afterwards.

Transfers: the cheapest time to add an abstraction is before you need it, and the only way that happens is if someone predicts the need. That prediction is most of what architecture is.

Four suspects, and I'd check them in this order because that's roughly the order of likelihood and ease.

Output length drift. Someone removed a length constraint, or a prompt change made the model chattier. Output is 3-5× the input price, so a doubling of average response length is a large bill change with no usage change. This is the most common cause and the easiest to fix.

A silent tier change. Someone switched to a reasoning model or a larger tier for a quality fix and nobody costed it. Reasoning tokens are invisible in the response but fully billed.

Cache breakage. A template edit moved a variable above the stable prefix, so we lost the cached-prefix discount on every call. That alone can be a 5-6× input cost swing, and nothing in the product looks different.

Conversation growth. If sessions got longer, each turn carries all previous turns. Token volume grows quadratically with turn count while request count grows linearly — which is exactly the 4×-vs-1.5× shape she's describing.

What I'd want to say next is the uncomfortable part: we can't answer this precisely unless we've been logging token counts per request, per prompt version. If we haven't, the first deliverable is that telemetry, and I'd rather admit it than produce a confident guess.

Transfers: when a cost grows faster than its apparent driver, look for a multiplier that changed — length, retries, fan-out, or history. Volume alone rarely explains a superlinear bill.

15Weigh open against closed on four real axes

The lecture frames open versus closed as a virtue: open source is "very good," it's the model he "likes very much." That's a preference, not an analysis, and it will not survive a design review.

Strip out the ideology and there are four axes that actually decide it.

Control. Open weights mean nobody can deprecate your model, change its behaviour overnight, or rate-limit you. That's genuine and underrated — it's the only way to get a model that behaves identically in three years. Closed means the vendor's roadmap is your roadmap.

Capability. The frontier is still closed, and the gap moves. Open weights are usually a generation behind on the hardest tasks and increasingly competitive on the ordinary ones. Since most production tasks are ordinary, this axis matters less than people assume.

Total cost. Discussed properly in the next section. The short version: the weights are free and the serving is not.

Risk transfer. The one nobody lists. A commercial API vendor typically carries some indemnity, provides a safety layer, patches issues, and has a support contract you can escalate through. With open weights, all of that becomes yours — including the safety filtering you didn't know the vendor was doing, and including liability, which some open-weight licences actively push back onto you.

16Cost the "free" model with actual GPU arithmetic

"You can download the weights and run it on your own machine." True, and it is the most expensive sentence in the lecture, because of what it leaves out.

Start with memory. A model's weights need roughly 2 bytes per parameter at half precision. A 70-billion-parameter model is therefore about 140 GB just to hold the weights — before any working memory. That does not fit on one GPU. You need multiple high-memory accelerators and the interconnect to make them act as one.

Then add the KV cache, which is the memory holding the attention state for every token in every active conversation. It scales with context length times batch size, and on long-context workloads it can rival the weights themselves. This is the line item nobody budgets and it's usually what makes the first capacity estimate wrong.

Quantisation helps a lot — 8-bit halves it, 4-bit quarters it — at some quality cost that you must measure rather than assume. And you can run a small model on a laptop very happily. But "run Llama on your own machine" and "serve a 70B model to 50,000 users" are separated by about two orders of magnitude of infrastructure.

The decisive variable is utilisation. A rented GPU bills 24 hours a day whether or not anyone is using it. An API bills only for tokens. So the break-even isn't about volume in the abstract — it's about how flat your traffic is.

17Filter by Indian regulatory constraints before you compare quality

The lecture chooses models on capability and openness. In an Indian regulated entity, capability is the last filter, not the first. Run the constraints first and you'll often find the choice was made for you.

RBI payment data localisation. End-to-end payment transaction data must be stored only in India. If that data appears in a prompt, any endpoint outside India is off the list. This alone eliminates the direct-vendor-API path for a large class of banking use cases.

DPDP Act 2023. You are the Data Fiduciary; the model provider is a Processor. You need a lawful basis, a stated purpose, a processing agreement, and a way to honour erasure requests — including data sitting in the vendor's logs. "Zero retention" in the contract is what makes erasure answerable.

RBI outsourcing and IT governance expectations. A model vendor is a service provider. That means due diligence, contractual audit rights, defined exit arrangements, and concentration-risk consideration. "We can't leave this vendor" is itself a finding.

Sectoral rules. SEBI for anything touching securities or investment advice, IRDAI for insurance claims and advertising. Both care about record-keeping and about who is accountable for a customer-facing statement.

18Build a scorecard, and put a review date on it

Model choice is not a decision you make once. It's a decision you re-make, quietly, every few months, and the only way to do that without re-arguing from scratch is to have written down why you chose last time.

A scorecard is a page. Weighted criteria, scores, a total, and the date. Its value is not the number at the bottom — it's that six months later you can see what changed. If a model was chosen because it was the only one in an Indian region, and three more arrive next quarter, that's a trigger to look again. Without the scorecard, nobody remembers the reason and the choice ossifies.

Weight the criteria before you score. Otherwise you'll score everything, notice the winner isn't the one you liked, and adjust the weights until it is. Doing weights first is a small act of intellectual honesty that saves a lot of argument.

Notice the top two rows are gates, not weighted criteria. A model that fails region availability or legal review doesn't get a low score — it doesn't get scored. Mixing hard constraints into a weighted average is how a non-compliant option wins on points and wastes a month.

Not on 4 points alone. The scorecard tells me it's worth evaluating, not that it's worth migrating — those are different thresholds and conflating them means switching constantly.

What I'd want against that 4 points: the migration cost. How many prompts need re-tuning, how much eval re-baselining, how long in shadow mode, and what's the rollback plan. For a mature integration that's typically weeks of work across several people. A 4-point gain rarely pays for that.

What would change my answer: if the gain is concentrated somewhere that matters disproportionately — a large jump on our worst-performing subgroup, or on Tamil, or a meaningful cost-per-request drop at our volume. A 4-point average hiding a 20-point improvement on the language where we're weakest is a different proposition entirely, and the aggregate score is exactly the thing that hides it.

What I'd actually do: run the new model against the golden set anyway — it's an afternoon — and record the result in the register with the date. If it's still ahead at the next review and our current snapshot is approaching end-of-life, we migrate then and get both changes for one migration cost.

Transfers: bundle migrations. The fixed cost of a switch is usually larger than the variable benefit, so wait until you have two reasons and do it once.

Because the initial choice has an owner and a deadline, and the review has neither. It's the classic shape of every "we'll revisit this periodically" commitment — a task with no forcing function doesn't happen, regardless of good intentions.

There's also a status asymmetry. Choosing is visible work with a decision attached; re-checking a decision that's working is invisible work whose best outcome is "no change." Nobody gets credit for confirming the status quo, so nobody volunteers.

What actually fixes it is attaching the review to something that already has a deadline. Snapshot end-of-life dates are perfect — they arrive whether you like it or not, they force a migration anyway, and you're re-testing everything at that moment regardless. Piggyback the scorecard refresh on the migration you're already doing. Second best: a calendar item with a named owner and a five-line output, so it costs an hour rather than a project.

Transfers: recurring reviews only survive if they're attached to an event with its own momentum. A date in a document is not a forcing function; a deprecation notice is.

19Assume every model you use will be retired

The lecture presents models as things that exist. They're better understood as things that are currently being served. Every hosted snapshot has an end-of-life, usually announced with a few months' notice, and after that the endpoint stops answering.

This is the structural consequence of the whole module. Model names decay in weeks; model availability decays in quarters. If your architecture assumes a specific model will still be there, you have built on something with a published expiry date and not written it down.

Three things follow. Pin snapshots but track their EOL. Pinning gives you control over when you migrate, not exemption from migrating. Put the dates in the same register as everything else. Budget a migration every six to twelve months, permanently. It belongs in run cost, not as a surprise project each time. Keep the eval suite alive. It's the only thing that converts a migration from a fortnight of anxiety into an afternoon of measurement.

Self-hosting changes the shape but not the existence of the problem. Nobody can take your downloaded weights away — but the ecosystem moves on. Serving-stack versions drop support, security patches stop, and in two years you're running something nobody optimises for. You've swapped a forced migration for a voluntary one you'll keep deferring.

Deprecation. The snapshot will eventually be retired and the endpoint will stop responding. Pinning converts an unpredictable change into a scheduled one — that's its whole value — but it doesn't make the model permanent.

So pinning and migration planning are a pair. Pin, record the EOL date next to it, and budget the migration. A pinned snapshot with no tracked expiry is an outage with a date on it that nobody has read.

Rule: every pinned dependency needs an owner and an expiry date in the same row.

By doing one before we're forced to, while the stakes are low.

Concretely: pick a non-critical internal feature and migrate it to a different model deliberately this quarter. Not because we need to — because the first migration is where we discover that our prompts are scattered across four repos, that nobody knows what "working" means for this feature, and that we have no rollback. Finding that out on an internal tool costs a week. Finding it out under a 90-day EOL notice on a customer-facing feature costs a lot more.

The artefacts that come out of that exercise are the actual deliverable: a golden set, a single client abstraction so the model is a config value, a documented ramp-and-rollback procedure, and a register with owners and dates. After that, migration is a runbook.

I'd also put it in the team's regular cadence rather than treating it as a project — the same way we handle certificate rotation or dependency upgrades. The framing matters: this isn't AI-specific drama, it's version management on a dependency with an unusually short support window.

Transfers: rehearse the recovery before you need it. Teams that have never restored a backup don't have backups, and teams that have never migrated a model don't have a migration path.

20The whole thing as one runnable script

The lecture ends by saying it will use the GPT and Claude APIs. Here is what that should look like once you take sections 09 through 19 seriously: a client where the model is a config value, not a code change.

It implements the tier ladder from §14, the snapshot pinning from §19, per-model token accounting from §08, cross-provider fallback, and the reasoning-before-verdict ordering from §03. Roughly 150 lines, and it is the difference between a migration being an afternoon and a fortnight.

CODE · PYTHONllm_client.py — provider-agnostic, with the ladder and the fallback
"""
One client. Model is configuration, not code.
pip install "anthropic>=0.40" "openai>=1.40" pydantic

The point of this file: when a snapshot hits EOL, or a cheaper
model appears, or Compliance says traffic must stay in India,
you edit a dict. You do not touch call sites.
"""
import os, time, json, logging
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Optional, Literal

log = logging.getLogger("llm")
Tier = Literal["small", "mid", "large"]

# --- 1. THE REGISTER --------------------------------------------------
# WHY: this dict is the "model register" from section 09. Every fact
# that decays in weeks lives HERE and nowhere else in the codebase.
# eol is not decoration - it is the thing nobody tracks (section 19).
@dataclass
class ModelSpec:
    provider: str
    snapshot: str            # DATED. never an alias.
    tier: Tier
    usd_in_per_mtok: float
    usd_out_per_mtok: float
    region: str
    eol: str                 # put it in the register or you will be surprised

REGISTER: dict[str, ModelSpec] = {
    "small": ModelSpec("anthropic", "claude-haiku-4-5-20251001",
                       "small", 1.00, 5.00, "ap-south-1", "2026-12-31"),
    "mid":   ModelSpec("anthropic", "claude-sonnet-4-5-20250929",
                       "mid",   3.00, 15.00, "ap-south-1", "2027-03-31"),
    "large": ModelSpec("openai",    "gpt-4.1-2025-04-14",
                       "large", 2.00, 8.00,  "us-east-1", "2026-11-30"),
}

# WHY a fallback map: providers have outages. A 529 should degrade
# to a working model, not to a 500 for your user.
FALLBACK: dict[str, Optional[str]] = {"large": "mid", "mid": "small", "small": None}

# --- 2. RESULT --------------------------------------------------------
@dataclass
class LLMResult:
    text: str
    snapshot: str
    tokens_in: int
    tokens_out: int
    usd: float
    latency_ms: int
    tier_used: str
    fell_back: bool = False

# --- 3. ADAPTERS ------------------------------------------------------
# WHY thin: the ONLY job here is to normalise the wire format.
# Resist putting business logic in an adapter - that is how you end up
# with provider-specific behaviour leaking into call sites.
def _call_anthropic(spec, system, user, max_tokens, temperature):
    from anthropic import Anthropic
    c = Anthropic()
    r = c.messages.create(
        model=spec.snapshot, max_tokens=max_tokens, temperature=temperature,
        # cache_control marks the stable prefix - see PE-01 section 11.
        system=[{"type": "text", "text": system,
                 "cache_control": {"type": "ephemeral"}}],
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": user}],
    )
    return r.content[0].text, r.usage.input_tokens, r.usage.output_tokens

def _call_openai(spec, system, user, max_tokens, temperature):
    from openai import OpenAI
    c = OpenAI()
    r = c.chat.completions.create(
        model=spec.snapshot, max_tokens=max_tokens, temperature=temperature,
        messages=[{"role": "system", "content": system},
                  {"role": "user",   "content": user}],
    )
    return (r.choices[0].message.content,
            r.usage.prompt_tokens, r.usage.completion_tokens)

ADAPTERS = {"anthropic": _call_anthropic, "openai": _call_openai}

# --- 4. THE ONE ENTRY POINT ------------------------------------------
def generate(system: str, user: str, tier: Tier = "mid",
             max_tokens: int = 1024, temperature: float = 0.0,
             india_only: bool = False) -> LLMResult:
    """Every LLM call in the codebase goes through here. No exceptions."""
    spec = REGISTER[tier]

    # WHY this check: section 17. Data class decides the endpoint BEFORE
    # quality does. Failing loudly here beats a compliance finding later.
    if india_only and not spec.region.startswith("ap-south"):
        raise PermissionError(
            f"tier={tier} runs in {spec.region}; payment data is India-only")

    t0 = time.time()
    try:
        text, tin, tout = ADAPTERS[spec.provider](
            spec, system, user, max_tokens, temperature)
        fell_back = False
    except Exception as e:
        nxt = FALLBACK.get(tier)
        if nxt is None:
            raise
        log.warning("tier=%s failed (%s); falling back to %s", tier, e, nxt)
        # Degrade, don't fail. A smaller model beats a 500 for the user.
        res = generate(system, user, nxt, max_tokens, temperature, india_only)
        res.fell_back = True
        return res

    usd = (tin * spec.usd_in_per_mtok + tout * spec.usd_out_per_mtok) / 1e6
    out = LLMResult(text, spec.snapshot, tin, tout, usd,
                    int((time.time() - t0) * 1000), tier, fell_back)

    # WHY log cost per call: section 14. You cannot answer "why did the
    # bill 4x?" from an invoice. You answer it from this line.
    log.info(json.dumps({"snapshot": out.snapshot, "tier": tier,
                         "in": tin, "out": tout, "usd": round(usd, 6),
                         "ms": out.latency_ms, "fell_back": fell_back}))
    return out

# --- 5. THE LADDER ----------------------------------------------------
# WHY: section 14. Cheap model first, escalate only on low confidence.
# Note the schema order: reasoning BEFORE verdict (section 03). If you
# swap these two keys the quality drops and nothing else looks different.
CLASSIFY_SYSTEM = """You route customer messages to a queue.
Return only JSON, no prose:
{"reasoning": str, "category": str, "confidence": "high"|"low"}
Categories: CARD_DISPUTE, LOAN_QUERY, KYC, NETBANKING, OTHER.
If two fit, pick the one with money at risk.
If unsure, use OTHER with confidence "low"."""

def classify(message: str) -> dict:
    r = generate(CLASSIFY_SYSTEM, message, tier="small", max_tokens=300)
    try:
        d = json.loads(r.text)
    except json.JSONDecodeError:
        d = {"confidence": "low"}          # bad JSON == low confidence

    if d.get("confidence") == "low":
        # Escalate. ~10% of traffic, ~12x the price, still far cheaper
        # than sending 100% to the large tier.
        r = generate(CLASSIFY_SYSTEM, message, tier="large", max_tokens=300)
        d = json.loads(r.text)
        d["escalated"] = True
    return d

if __name__ == "__main__":
    logging.basicConfig(level=logging.INFO, format="%(message)s")
    for m in ["charged twice for the same order yesterday",
              "my EMI didn't go through and now the app says something weird"]:
        print(json.dumps(classify(m), indent=2))

They're right about the general principle and wrong about this specific case, and it's worth explaining why rather than overruling.

Premature abstraction is a real cost, and most of the time "you might need it later" is a bad argument. What makes this different is that we know the change is coming. Snapshots have published end-of-life dates. The pricing changes quarterly. The tier ladder is how the vendors have structured their own products. This isn't speculative flexibility — it's a scheduled event we can see on a calendar.

And the cost asymmetry is stark. Writing this file now is an afternoon. Retrofitting it across forty call sites, under a 90-day EOL notice, with no eval suite to tell you what broke, is a fortnight and a risk. That's not a close call.

Where I'd concede ground: don't build the fallback chain, the router, and the cost dashboard on day one. Build the single entry point — that's the part that's expensive to add later. Everything else can be added inside it whenever it's needed, precisely because there's one place to add it.

Transfers: the test for premature abstraction isn't "will we need it?" It's "is the cost of adding it later much higher than now?" For choke points, it almost always is.

Glossary

Every term used above, one plain sentence each.

Adapter
A thin wrapper that normalises one vendor's API into your own internal call shape.
Aggregator
A platform like Bedrock, Vertex Model Garden or Azure AI Foundry that serves many vendors' models through one API inside your own cloud account.
Alias (model)
An undated model name that the vendor can silently re-point to different weights.
Apache 2.0
A permissive open-source licence allowing commercial use, modification and redistribution, with a patent grant.
Autoregression
Generating each token using all previous tokens as input, which is why the model cannot revise what it has already said.
Bedrock
AWS's managed service for calling third-party and Amazon models from inside your own AWS account and region.
Chain-of-thought
Prompting the model to write its reasoning before its answer, which buys it more computation and external working memory.
Closed model
A model whose weights are not published; you reach it only through an API under the vendor's terms.
Community licence
A vendor's custom licence that publishes weights but attaches conditions — the category Llama falls into.
Decoding
The process of turning the model's probability distribution into an actual chosen token.
DPDP Act 2023
India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, governing lawful basis, purpose limitation and erasure of personal data.
Embedding
The vector a token ID is converted into before the model's layers process it.
EOL
End of life — the date a model snapshot stops being served.
Greedy decoding
Always choosing the single highest-probability token; repeatable, and prone to flat, looping text.
Golden set
A small fixed collection of real inputs with known-good outputs, used to detect regressions on every prompt or model change.
Hugging Face
The main public hub for model weights, hosting every licence tier behind an identical download button.
Indemnity
A contractual promise to cover someone's losses; commercial API vendors often provide some, open-weight licences usually don't.
KV cache
GPU memory holding attention state for every token in every active conversation; the sizing line teams forget.
Logits
The raw scores the model produces for every token in its vocabulary before they are turned into probabilities.
Localisation (data)
A legal requirement that data be stored only within a country; RBI mandates this for payment transaction data.
Model register
A one-page table recording each model you call with its snapshot, licence, region, price, eval score, EOL date and owner.
Nucleus sampling
See top-p.
On-device
Running a small model directly on a phone or laptop so data never leaves the hardware.
Open weights
Weights you can download and run, under whatever licence the publisher chose — which may or may not be open source.
OSI
The Open Source Initiative, the body that defines what counts as open source and has said Llama's licence does not qualify.
Quantisation
Storing weights at lower precision to cut memory and cost, at some measurable quality loss.
Reasoning model
A tier trained to generate a long hidden scratchpad before answering; higher quality on hard tasks, much higher output cost.
SBOM
Software bill of materials — the inventory of your dependencies, which should now include model weights.
Scratchpad
Text the model generates to work through a problem before committing to an answer.
Self-consistency
Sampling the same question several times and checking whether answers agree, as a cheap uncertainty signal.
Snapshot
A specific dated model version whose behaviour is fixed until the vendor retires it.
Softmax
The function that converts raw logits into probabilities that sum to one.
Subword tokenization
Splitting text into word fragments so any string can be represented from a fixed vocabulary.
Temperature
The knob that flattens or sharpens the next-token distribution before sampling.
Tier ladder
The small / mid / large / reasoning family every vendor ships, mirroring the difficulty distribution of real traffic.
Token
The chunk of text a model reads as one unit and the unit you are billed in — an integer, not a word.
Tokenizer
The fixed lookup table that splits text into tokens; different models use different ones, so token counts differ.
Top-k
Keeping only the k highest-probability tokens before sampling.
Top-p
Keeping the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities sum to p, so the candidate pool adapts to the model's uncertainty.
Utilisation
How busy your GPUs actually are; the variable that decides whether self-hosting beats an API.
Vertex AI
Google Cloud's platform for running Google and third-party models in your own project and region.
Vocabulary
The fixed set of tokens a model can read and write, typically 100,000 to 200,000 entries.
vLLM
A widely used open-source serving stack for running open-weight models efficiently on your own GPUs.
Zero retention
A contract term under which the vendor does not store your prompts or responses after serving the request.
