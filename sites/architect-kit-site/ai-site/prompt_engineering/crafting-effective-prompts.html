The course teaches two rules — be clear, give context — as if they were separate skills. They are the same operation performed on opposite ends of the pipe: closing degrees of freedom. What you leave open, the model fills in, and it will fill it in plausibly rather than correctly.

Reading contract

By the end you can: name the eight dimensions every prompt either pins or gambles on; convert an unmeasurable adjective into a checkable constraint; tell the difference between context that helps and context that only costs tokens; and prove — with an ablation, not an opinion — which clause of your prompt is actually doing the work.

Marking convention

beyond transcript means the lecture never said this. correction means I am contradicting the lecture on purpose.

The lecture gives you two sessions and two rules. Useful, but it hides the structure. Both rules do exactly one thing: they remove choices the model would otherwise make on your behalf.

The difference is only which end of the pipe they act on. Clarity closes the output side — how many, how long, what shape, what tone. Context closes the input side — who is asking, what this material is, who will read the result, where it will end up. Same operation. Opposite ends.

   who is asking                                    how many items
   what this material is                            how long
   who will read the answer                         what shape
   where the answer will be used                    what register
   what may be assumed known                        what to do at the edges

              │  THE MODEL PINS FOR YOU — using the │
              │  most statistically ordinary value  │

The reframe worth keeping

Stop asking "is this prompt clear?" — you wrote it, so it is clear to you. Ask instead: "what will the model have to invent?" That question is answerable by inspection, it is answerable by someone else on your team, and it converts prompt review from taste into a checklist.

The eight dimensions

Almost every prompt failure I have traced back to a wording problem was actually one of these left open. Not all eight matter every time — but you should be choosing to leave one open, not discovering later that you did.

DimensionThe questionWhat the model defaults to if you skip it
TaskWhat verb? Summarise, explain, classify, rewrite, critique?A blended answer that does a bit of several
ScopeHow much of the subject is in bounds?Everything it knows, in encyclopaedia order
AudienceWho reads this, with what prior knowledge?A generic literate adult with no context
FormProse, bullets, table, dialogue, code?Headed sections with bullets — the house style of the internet
LengthHow many words, items, sentences?Medium. Always medium.
RegisterFormal, warm, dry, playful?Mildly enthusiastic corporate neutral
GroundingAnswer from what — the pasted text, or its own memory?Both, mixed together, unmarked
DestinationWhere does this output go next?Nowhere — so nothing is tuned for the real use
Why the defaults are so consistent beyond transcript

An unpinned dimension is not filled randomly. The model resolves it toward the most typical value in its training distribution for that kind of request. That is why unspecified prompts across the whole world converge on the same shape — headings, three-to-five bullets, a summary sentence, cautious optimism. Your generic output is not a bug in your prompt. It is the average of the internet, which is exactly what you asked for.

Active recall

You ask for "a short professional summary of this incident report" and get something usable but wrong in tone. Which dimensions did you actually pin, and which did you only appear to?

Pinned: task (summarise) and grounding (this report). Only apparently pinned: length — "short" is a feeling, not a number; register — "professional" covers everything from a legal filing to a LinkedIn post. Entirely unpinned: audience, form, scope, destination. The tone complaint is really a destination complaint: a summary for the on-call engineer and one for the regulator are different documents, and you named neither.

Correction correction

"Tell me about Ottawa" is perfectly clear. It is an unambiguous instruction, and the model executed it faithfully — it told you about Ottawa. What it lacked was not clarity. It lacked scope, form, length and audience. The distinction matters because "be clearer" gives you nothing to do, while "you left four dimensions open" tells you exactly what to write next.

Active recall

Why is "be more specific" bad advice to give a junior on your team?

Because it names a direction without naming an action, so they will add words rather than close dimensions — usually adjectives, which close nothing. "Which of the eight dimensions did you leave to the model, and which of those do you actually care about?" is the same advice made executable, and it produces a shorter prompt as often as a longer one.

beyond transcript This is the single most common way an experienced person writes a bad prompt. You add a word that carries a strong preference in your head and no information in the prompt. It feels specified. Nothing was specified.

The test is simple: could two competent people disagree about whether the output satisfies it? If yes, it is a preference, not a constraint — and the model is now guessing at your taste.

Trap

The right-hand column is longer, and people resist it for that reason. But look at what you get: every one of those is something you or a script can verify after the fact. The left-hand column can only be reviewed by re-reading and feeling vaguely dissatisfied — which is exactly how prompts end up being tweaked forever without improving.

When you genuinely cannot specify it beyond transcript

Sometimes you know the thing you want when you see it and cannot describe it — house voice, a particular dryness, the right level of hedging. Do not keep rewording. Prompt by discrimination instead of specification: ask for three deliberately different versions, pick the one that is right, then work backwards and write down what made it right. That written-down thing is your constraint, and you now have it in words. Half the time you also discover the example is easier to paste than the rule — which is the moment to reach for one-shot prompting.

The lecture says: add a fourth constraint, a fifth, and so on. Presented as monotonic — more constraints, better output. It is not monotonic, and the failure is quiet.

Correction correction

Constraints compete for the same output budget. Three bullet points, 100 words, for kids, covering the full history of the city, with dates and an analogy each — that specification has no satisfying solution. The model will not tell you so. It silently drops the constraint it judges least important and returns something that looks fine until you check.

Counts are requests, not guarantees

"Exactly 100 words" is followed approximately. Models do not count reliably while generating — length emerges from the shape of the text, not from a counter. Two practical consequences: give a range rather than a point ("80–120 words"), because a range is achievable and a point invites padding; and if the limit is load-bearing — an SMS, a meta description, a UI string — validate it in code and regenerate. A constraint that costs money when violated does not belong solely in a prompt.

Active recall

Your prompt has nine constraints. Outputs are inconsistent — sometimes constraint 3 is honoured, sometimes constraint 7. Nothing is obviously contradictory. What do you do first?

Assume they are contradictory and you have not spotted it — inconsistent honouring across calls is the signature of an over-subscribed budget, not of a weak model. Work out the implied cost of each constraint against your length limit. Then rank them explicitly and state the tie-break rule in the prompt. If nothing conflicts on inspection, the second move is ablation: drop one clause at a time and score, which will show you which pair is fighting. There is a harness for that in section 4.2.

The lecture treats context as one thing — "who the user is, what role the model plays, what data it's looking at, what task you want" — and then demonstrates all four at once. Bundled together they look like a single trick. Separated, they have very different value per token, and you should know which one you are buying.

Who actually earned the credit correction

In the magazine-editor demo the lecturer changes four things at once and attributes the improvement to the role. Look at the two prompts again: the good one added a role and an audience and a format and a topic label. On a summarisation task, "for high school students, in three bullet points" is doing nearly all of the work, and "you are an editor of a science magazine" is doing very little. This is not a matter of opinion — it is a two-minute ablation, and section 4.2 runs it. Change one clause at a time or you will spend years carrying clauses that do nothing.

Active recall

You have budget for exactly one extra sentence of context in a prompt that rewrites internal announcements. Which do you spend it on?

Destination, and it should carry the audience with it: "This will be posted in a company-wide Slack channel and read on phones by people who were not in the meeting." That one sentence settles length, formality, whether context must be restated, whether a call to action is needed, and whether jargon from the meeting can survive. A role sentence would have settled only the vocabulary.

"Add context" is advice that scales badly, because the obvious way to follow it — write more preamble — hits three problems the lecture never mentions.

Problem one: describing data is not providing data

beyond transcript This is the important one. "This is an abstract on load forecasting" is a label. Pasting the abstract is grounding. The lecture's examples always paste the text, so the distinction never surfaces — and then people write prompts like "considering our refund policy, answer this customer" and wonder why the answer is invented. The model has no refund policy. It has a sentence claiming one exists.

   LABEL                                GROUND
   "considering our refund policy…"     <policy>
                                          Refunds within 30 days of
                                          delivery. Opened items:
                                          store credit only. …
                                        </policy>

   model has: a topic hint              model has: the actual rule
   model does: invent a plausible       model does: apply the rule,
               policy                                or say it is silent

   TEST: delete the label from your prompt. If nothing about the
   answer could change, you wrote a label. Labels cost tokens and
   buy framing. Ground costs tokens and buys correctness.

Problem two: context the model can already see

Telling a model "the following is a scientific abstract" when you have pasted a scientific abstract is close to free of information — it can see that. Spend those tokens on what is not recoverable from the input: who will read the output, why they care, what they will do with it, what they already believe that is wrong. That last one is the highest-value context sentence in existence and almost nobody writes it.

The rule

Context earns its tokens when it is not inferable from the input and not inferable from the task. Everything else is padding that pushes your real instruction further from the end of the prompt.

Problem three: burying the instruction

A long preamble puts your actual request in the middle of the prompt, which is the position models attend to least reliably. If your context block runs long, restate the task in one line at the very bottom. It costs eight words and it measurably improves compliance on long prompts.

# BURIED — task stated once, in the middle, under 300 words of setup
You are an editor at a science magazine. Our readership skews
young... [long preamble] ...and we have historically found that
readers disengage when we use equations.
Summarise the abstract below.
[500-word abstract]

# ANCHORED — same words, task stated at the top AND restated last
Summarise the abstract below for our readers.

<context>
You are an editor at a science magazine. Readers are 13-17...
[the same preamble, now clearly marked as setting, not instruction]
</context>

<abstract>
[500-word abstract]
</abstract>

Three bullet points. No equations. 100-140 words total.
Active recall

A teammate's prompt opens with 200 words about the company's mission and values before asking the model to categorise support tickets. Is that context or padding?

Padding, on this task. Apply the deletion test: remove it and ask whether any ticket would be categorised differently. Mission statements do not change a label. It costs tokens on every call, pushes the real instruction into the low-attention middle, and — if it sits in the cached prefix and someone edits a comma — invalidates the cache. The context that would pay here is the label definitions and a boundary example for the two labels people confuse.

Rewrite "Explain photosynthesis" for students aged 13–17. The output must be accurate, age-appropriate and engaging. The course says a good answer settles three questions: who is the audience, what tone, what format — and that any prompt answering all three is correct.

Here is a prompt that satisfies the rubric completely.

You are a science teacher writing for students aged 13-17.

Correction correction

The course concludes: no unique answer, and if you answered the three questions your prompt is correct. The first half is true. The second half is where the exercise stops being an exercise and starts being wrong about the job. "No unique answer" does not mean "no better answer." Two prompts can both satisfy that rubric and produce outputs that differ enormously in quality — and the prompt above has four specific holes that will show up in the output every time.

#HoleWhat you get
1"13–17" is not an audienceIt is a four-year span across the biggest change in scientific vocabulary a student ever undergoes. A 13-year-old may not have met a chemical equation; a 17-year-old may be revising for board exams. The model will average them and satisfy neither.
2No accuracy contractThe brief demands accuracy, but every explanation for teenagers simplifies. Which simplifications are allowed? Unstated, so the model picks the most common ones on the internet — which include the famous wrong ones.
3"Engaging" is unmeasurableYou get exclamation marks and the word "amazing". That is the model's best guess at engaging, because it is the average of every children's science page ever written.
4No length, no verificationNothing states how long, and nothing checks whether the result is actually readable by a 13-year-old rather than merely described as being for one.
Hole 2 is the interesting one beyond transcript

Ask any model for photosynthesis "for kids" and you will very often get: plants make their food from sunlight (omitting that the carbon comes from CO₂ — the actual point); plants breathe in CO₂ and breathe out oxygen as a tidy exchange (implying respiration doesn't happen in plants, which it does, constantly); and chlorophyll "absorbs sunlight" with no mention that it reflects green, which is why leaves look green. These are not the model being careless. They are the most common phrasings in the source material, and an unspecified prompt reproduces the most common phrasing. If accuracy matters, name the specific errors you are refusing. That is one sentence and it is worth more than every tone instruction combined.

Same task. Every dimension from section 0.1 either pinned or deliberately left open, an accuracy contract, engagement operationalised, and a check that does not rely on the model's own judgement.

# TASK
Explain photosynthesis.

# AUDIENCE  (narrowed — a 4-year band is not an audience)
Students aged 13-14, first year of secondary science.
Assume they know: living things need energy; air contains
different gases; plants have leaves and roots.
Assume they do NOT know: chemical equations, the words
molecule, glucose, chloroplast, or respiration.
Define any word outside that list in the same sentence you use it.

# ACCURACY CONTRACT  (the clause that does the real work)
Simplify freely, but these four things must be right:
- The carbon in the plant's food comes from carbon dioxide in
  the air, not from the soil and not from sunlight.
- Sunlight supplies the energy; it is not an ingredient.
- Leaves look green because chlorophyll reflects green light
  and absorbs red and blue.
- Plants also use oxygen and release carbon dioxide, all the
  time. Photosynthesis is not the reverse of breathing.
Do not write that plants "make food out of sunlight" and do
not present gas exchange as a simple one-way swap.

# FORM AND LENGTH
Four short paragraphs, 30-45 words each. No bullet points,
no headings, no equations, no bold text.

# REGISTER  ("engaging", made checkable)
- Open with a question the reader can answer from memory.
- Include exactly one everyday analogy, drawn from cooking,
  building, or money - not from factories or machines.
- Include one fact most 13-year-olds would find surprising.
- No exclamation marks. Do not use the words amazing,
  incredible, fascinating or journey.

# SELF-CHECK  (cheap, and catches the common failure)
Before answering, list any word you used that is not in the
"assume they know" list and is not defined in place. If that
list is not empty, rewrite first. Do not show me the list.
What changed, and why it is worth the extra words

Every instruction above is verifiable by someone who is not you. Word counts, paragraph counts, a banned-word list, a named set of misconceptions, a defined vocabulary floor. A second person can review the output against the prompt and reach the same verdict you would — which is the actual definition of a good specification, and the thing "make it engaging" can never give you.

Active recall

Your explainer scores grade 7.2 on Flesch–Kincaid, uses no banned words, and hits every length target. A teacher reads it and says students won't follow it. Who is wrong?

Nobody. Flesch–Kincaid counts syllables per word and words per sentence — it measures surface simplicity, not conceptual load. "The energy is stored in the bonds that hold the parts of the sugar to each other" is short-worded, short-sentenced, and completely opaque to someone who has never thought about bonds. The metric is a cheap regression guard, not a definition of comprehensibility. Keep it for catching drift; keep the teacher for deciding whether it works.

Two caveats you should state out loud before anyone quotes your numbers

Flesch–Kincaid measures syllables per word and words per sentence. That is all. It cannot see that a short sentence contains an idea the reader has never met. Use it as a regression guard — "grade level moved from 7 to 12 after that edit" is a real signal — never as a definition of comprehensibility.


A model scoring another model's output is not ground truth. It correlates with human judgement, which makes it useful for triage across hundreds of outputs. It also has known biases: toward longer answers, toward answers that sound like its own writing, and toward whichever option is listed first. Calibrate it against human scores on a sample before you trust its verdicts, and re-calibrate when you change models.














Your prompt has eight clauses. Which ones matter? Almost every prompt in production carries clauses that do nothing — inherited from a blog post, added during a bad afternoon, never removed because removing things feels risky. The method for finding them is old, simple, and takes minutes.


Leave one out. Build the full prompt, then build one variant per clause with that clause deleted. Run each over the same inputs. Score each. The drop in score when a clause is removed is that clause's contribution.

The specific experiment this module owes you

Run it on the lecture's own magazine-editor prompt. Four clauses: role, audience, format, topic label. My prediction — and it is a prediction, not a claim, which is the point of running it — is that audience and format carry nearly all of the improvement and the role clause lands under Δ 0.03. The script in the next part does exactly this. If I am wrong, you will find out in about ninety seconds, which is far better than either of us being confident.






Two conditions or the numbers are noise

Temperature at 0, or you are measuring sampling variation rather than clause contribution. And more than one input — a single item makes every Δ either 0 or 1. Ten to twenty inputs is enough to rank clauses, which is all you need; it is not enough to publish.






Active recall

Ablation says your "you are a world-class expert" clause has Δ 0.00. A colleague objects that it obviously helps and everyone uses it. What is the strongest version of their objection, and does it survive?



The strongest version: your rubric may only measure what is easy to measure — length, structure, banned words — and the role clause may be improving something the rubric is blind to, like how the prose actually reads. That objection is legitimate and it is a criticism of your rubric, not a defence of the clause. It survives only if they can name the quality being missed and add a check for it. If they cannot name it, the clause is decoration, and decoration in a cached prefix is decoration you pay for on every call forever.

# Several subjects, because a single input makes every delta 0 or 1.
SUBJECTS = [
    "photosynthesis",
    "how vaccines work",
    "why the sky is blue",
    "how a fridge makes things cold",
]

BANNED = ("amazing", "incredible", "fascinating", "journey")
JARGON = ("molecule", "glucose", "chloroplast", "respiration")

# Phrasings that indicate a known misconception survived. Crude on purpose:
# a keyword pass is a smoke alarm, not a fire inspection. It catches the
# common wording, and a human still reads the finalists.
MISCONCEPTIONS = (
    "food from sunlight",
    "food out of sunlight",
    "food from the sun",
    "energy from the soil",
    "absorbs green",
)


def build(skip: str | None, subject: str) -> str:
    """Assemble the prompt, optionally omitting one named clause."""
    body = [v for k, v in CLAUSES.items() if k != skip]
    # Task first and restated last: a long prompt buries a middle
    # instruction, and eight words at the bottom measurably fix that.
    return (f"Explain {subject}.\n\n" + "\n\n".join(body) +
            f"\n\nNow explain {subject}. Output the explanation alone.")


# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. SCORING - only things a machine can decide
#
# Every check below is objective. Nothing here judges whether the writing
# is good; that is what the twenty-minute human read is for. The point of
# this rubric is to catch DRIFT cheaply, on every change, forever.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
def syllables(word: str) -> int:
    """Vowel-group heuristic. Wrong on maybe 5% of English words, which is
    fine for a relative measure and useless for an absolute claim."""
    w = re.sub(r"[^a-z]", "", word.lower())
    if not w:
        return 0
    groups = re.findall(r"[aeiouy]+", w)
    count = len(groups)
    if w.endswith("e") and not w.endswith(("le", "ee")) and count > 1:
        count -= 1          # silent terminal e
    return max(count, 1)


def flesch_kincaid(text: str) -> float:
    """US grade level. Measures syllables per word and words per sentence -
    NOT conceptual difficulty. A regression guard, not a definition."""
    sentences = [s for s in re.split(r"[.!?]+", text) if s.strip()]
    words = re.findall(r"[A-Za-z']+", text)
    if not sentences or not words:
        return 0.0
    syl = sum(syllables(w) for w in words)
    return (0.39 * len(words) / len(sentences)
            + 11.8 * syl / len(words) - 15.59)


def score(text: str) -> float:
    """Six equally weighted checks. Equal weighting is a choice, not a
    truth - reweight it to match what actually costs you when it fails."""
    low = text.lower()
    paras = [p for p in text.split("\n\n") if p.strip()]
    counts = [len(p.split()) for p in paras]

    checks = [
        len(paras) == 4,
        bool(counts) and all(30 <= c <= 45 for c in counts),
        not any(b in low for b in BANNED),
        not any(j in low for j in JARGON),
        not any(m in low for m in MISCONCEPTIONS),
        6.0 <= flesch_kincaid(text) <= 9.0,
    ]
    return sum(checks) / len(checks)


# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. THE ABLATION
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
@dataclass
class Variant:
    label: str
    skip: str | None
    mean: float = 0.0


def ask(prompt: str) -> str:
    resp = client.messages.create(
        model=MODEL,
        max_tokens=MAX_TOKENS,
        temperature=TEMPERATURE,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": prompt}],
    )
    return resp.content[0].text


def run() -> None:
    if not os.environ.get("ANTHROPIC_API_KEY"):
        raise SystemExit("Set ANTHROPIC_API_KEY first.")

    variants = [Variant("FULL PROMPT", None)]
    variants += [Variant(f"- {name}", name) for name in CLAUSES]

    for v in variants:
        scores = []
        for subject in SUBJECTS:
            scores.append(score(ask(build(v.skip, subject))))
        v.mean = sum(scores) / len(scores)
        print(f"ran {v.label}")

    baseline = variants[0].mean
    print(f"\nmodel: {MODEL}   subjects: {len(SUBJECTS)}   temp: {TEMPERATURE}\n")
    print(f"{'variant':<18}{'score':>8}{'delta':>8}   verdict")
    print("-" * 58)

    for v in variants:
        if v.skip is None:
            print(f"{v.label:<18}{v.mean:>8.2f}{'':>8}   baseline")
            continue
        delta = baseline - v.mean
        if delta <= 0.02:
            verdict = "DEAD - delete it"
        elif delta < 0.10:
            verdict = "marginal - keep only if failure is costly"
        else:
            verdict = "LOAD-BEARING - protect it"
        print(f"{v.label:<18}{v.mean:>8.2f}{delta:>8.2f}   {verdict}")

    print("\nA negative delta means removing the clause HELPED. Rare, real,"
          "\nand always worth the ten minutes to investigate.")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    run()





What I changed from the course material, and why






ChangeReason



The prompt is a dict of named clauses, not a stringA prompt kept as one blob cannot be ablated, diffed clause-by-clause, or reasoned about. This costs nothing and makes the whole experiment a for-loop.


Four subjects, not oneThe lectures evaluate on a single example. With one input every delta is 0 or 1 and the ranking is meaningless.


Temperature pinned to 0Never mentioned in the transcripts. At default temperature an ablation measures sampling noise as much as clause contribution.


Reading level computed locally, with the caveat in the docstringIt is the only cheap objective proxy for "age-appropriate", and the caveat belongs next to the code so nobody quotes the number as comprehension.


Misconception keywords, not just tone checksThe exercise demands accuracy and then measures none of it. This is crude — deliberately labelled as a smoke alarm — but a crude accuracy check beats an eloquent tone check.


Task restated at the end of the promptLong prompts bury the middle. Eight words at the bottom is the cheapest compliance fix available.


Verdict strings, not just numbersA table of deltas gets admired. A column that says "DEAD — delete it" gets acted on.

























LLM
Large Language Model. A model trained to predict the next token over very large text corpora.


Token
The unit a model reads and writes — roughly ¾ of an English word. Cost, context limits and latency are all measured in these.


Degree of freedom
A dimension of the output your prompt does not fix, and which the model therefore fixes for you.


Constraint
A requirement two competent reviewers would agree on. If they could disagree, it is a preference, not a constraint.


Grounding
Placing the actual source material in the prompt, as opposed to describing that it exists.


Destination
Where the output will be used. The highest-compression context sentence available, because it silently settles length, register, hedging and structure at once.


Ablation
Removing one component at a time and re-scoring, to measure what each component contributes.


Leave-one-out
The specific ablation pattern used here: one variant per clause, each missing exactly that clause.


Flesch–Kincaid
A readability formula returning a US grade level from syllables per word and words per sentence. Measures surface complexity only.


LLM-as-judge
Using a model to score another model's output against a rubric. Useful for triage, biased toward length and toward its own style, and never ground truth.


Golden set
A small fixed set of inputs with known-good properties, re-run on every prompt or model change.


Prompt caching
Provider-side reuse of a byte-identical prompt prefix at a fraction of input price. Any edit to the prefix invalidates it.


Temperature
The sampling parameter controlling randomness. 0 is near-deterministic and is required for any prompt comparison to mean anything.


One-shot prompting
Including a single worked example. Reach for it when the target is easier to show than to describe.
