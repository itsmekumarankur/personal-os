LLM Evaluation & Safety

An LLM that "seems to work" and an LLM you can defend in front of a compliance team are two very different bars. This part is about closing that gap.

§ 10.1 — WHY EVALUATION IS HARD
Thought experiment

You've tried a prompt on ten questions and it nailed all ten. A colleague changes one word in the system prompt and tries the same ten — also perfect. Whose version is actually better? If your only evaluation method is "I tried it and it looked right," can you even answer that question?

The core problem: non-determinism at scale

Traditional software testing assumes a deterministic function: same input, same output, assert equality. LLMs break that assumption at every level — the same prompt can produce different outputs across runs (temperature > 0), "correct" often has more than one valid phrasing, and a change that helps 80% of cases can silently regress the other 20%. This is why LLM evaluation needs to be treated as its own engineering discipline, not an afterthought bolted onto unit tests.

Three complementary evaluation approaches exist, each with a different cost/signal tradeoff, and production systems typically use all three at different stages:

  • Benchmarks — fast, cheap, reproducible, but generic and easy to overfit to.
  • LLM-as-judge — scalable, flexible to your own task, but inherits the judge model's own biases and blind spots.
  • Human evaluation — the actual ground truth for subjective quality, but slow and expensive, so it's used sparingly and strategically.
§ 10.2 — BENCHMARKS
Thought experiment

A model scores 92% on a widely-cited reasoning benchmark. Should that number alone convince you it will perform well on your company's internal compliance Q&A task — a task the benchmark's authors have never seen or imagined?

What public benchmarks measure — and their limits

BenchmarkMeasuresCategory
MMLUBroad academic/professional knowledge across 57 subjectsKnowledge
GSM8K / MATHGrade-school to competition-level math reasoningReasoning
HumanEval / MBPPCode generation correctness (unit-test pass rate)Coding
HellaSwagCommonsense sentence completionCommonsense reasoning

Why benchmarks alone mislead

  • Contamination — benchmark questions and answers can leak into training data, inflating scores without real capability gains.
  • Benchmark-task mismatch — a model excelling at MMLU trivia questions tells you almost nothing about its ability to follow your company's specific tone-of-voice or ground answers in your retrieved documents.
  • Saturation — top models now cluster near the ceiling on older benchmarks, making them poor discriminators between "good" and "great" models.
  • Static snapshots — a benchmark score is a single point-in-time measurement; it says nothing about a model's stability across your actual production input distribution.
The practical takeaway Public benchmarks are useful for coarse model selection ("is this new model even in the right ballpark") — they are not a substitute for a task-specific eval set built from your own real or representative queries. Building even 50-100 hand-curated examples that mirror your actual use case tells you more than any public leaderboard.
§ 10.3 — LLM-AS-JUDGE
Thought experiment

If a model can be fooled by a misleading question, why would you trust that same class of model to reliably judge whether another model's answer is good? Isn't the judge just as capable of being wrong as the model it's grading?

Using a strong LLM to grade outputs at scale

LLM-as-judge uses a (usually more capable, or simply a different) LLM to score or compare generated outputs against a rubric — enabling evaluation at a scale that human review can't match, and unlocking automated regression testing across thousands of examples on every prompt/model change.

Common judging patterns

  • Pointwise scoring — judge rates a single response on a rubric (e.g. 1-5 for helpfulness, faithfulness, tone).
  • Pairwise comparison — judge is shown two responses (e.g. old prompt vs new prompt) and picks the better one; generally more reliable than absolute pointwise scores because relative judgments are easier for LLMs to make consistently.
  • Reference-based grading — judge compares a generated answer against a known-correct reference answer, checking factual alignment rather than free-form quality.
  • Rubric/criteria-based grading — judge checks a checklist of specific criteria (e.g. "does the answer cite a source," "does it avoid giving investment advice") rather than a single vague score.

Known biases of LLM judges (know these before you trust the numbers)

BiasWhat it means
Position biasIn pairwise comparisons, the judge favors whichever answer appears first (or second) — mitigated by evaluating both orderings and averaging
Verbosity biasLonger answers are rated as "better" even when not more correct or useful
Self-preference biasA model judge tends to rate outputs from its own model family more favorably
Style-over-substanceConfident, well-formatted, fluent answers score higher even when factually wrong
Trap to avoid Never use the exact same model for both generation and judging without validating the judge against human labels first. Calibrate any LLM-judge pipeline against a small human-labeled gold set periodically — treat the judge's agreement rate with humans as its own metric to monitor, not as a fixed assumption.

§ 10.4 — HUMAN EVAL
Thought experiment

If automated evals can run continuously and at near-zero marginal cost, is there anything a slow, expensive human reviewer can catch that an LLM judge structurally cannot?

Where humans remain irreplaceable

Human evaluation is the calibration anchor for everything else — LLM-judges are only trustworthy insofar as they're periodically validated against human judgment. Humans catch things judges systematically miss: subtle domain expertise errors (a wrong-but-plausible-sounding financial claim), cultural/tonal appropriateness, genuinely novel failure modes the judge prompt never anticipated, and real end-user satisfaction that a rubric can't fully encode.

Structuring human eval so it's actually useful

  • Clear rubrics, not vague ratings — "rate 1-5" without criteria produces noisy, inconsistent labels; break it into specific yes/no or scaled criteria (grounded? on-topic? correct tone? safe?).
  • Inter-rater reliability — have multiple annotators label a sample of the same items and measure agreement (e.g. Cohen's Kappa); low agreement means your rubric itself is ambiguous, not that annotators are careless.
  • Pairwise/Elo-style comparison — similar to LLM-judge pairwise, but with humans; used by leaderboards like Chatbot Arena to rank models via head-to-head human preference votes, aggregated into an Elo rating.
  • Sampling strategically — you can't human-review everything in production; sample disproportionately from low-confidence outputs, user complaints/thumbs-down, and edge cases rather than a uniform random sample.
The practical hierarchy Use benchmarks to sanity-check a new model quickly → build an LLM-judge pipeline for continuous, scaled regression testing on your own task → periodically spend human review time on a targeted sample to calibrate the judge and catch what it structurally can't see.
§ 10.5 — HALLUCINATION DETECTION
Thought experiment

A model gives you a fluent, well-structured, entirely wrong answer about a regulatory clause — with the same confident tone it uses when it's completely correct. If the model itself gives you no signal that it's unsure, how could you possibly catch the error automatically, before a user does?

Catching fabrication before it reaches a user

First, a distinction that matters: intrinsic hallucination is when the output contradicts the given source/context (a faithfulness failure); extrinsic hallucination is when the output states something unverifiable against the source — not necessarily false, but not supported either. Detection techniques differ by which one you're targeting.

Detection techniques

  • Self-consistency sampling — generate the same answer multiple times (at temperature > 0) and check agreement; low agreement across samples is a strong signal of uncertainty/fabrication, since a truly known fact tends to be reproduced consistently.
  • SelfCheckGPT-style methods — sample multiple responses and check whether they're mutually consistent using NLI (natural language inference) or similarity scoring, without needing any external reference — the intuition being that hallucinated details vary across samples while grounded facts stay stable.
  • NLI/entailment-based faithfulness checking — for RAG-grounded answers specifically, run an entailment model (or LLM-judge) to check whether each claim in the answer is entailed by the retrieved context, not merely topically related to it.
  • Uncertainty/logprob signals — when token-level log-probabilities are available, low-confidence tokens (the model was "unsure" at generation time) correlate with higher hallucination risk, though this signal is weaker for API-only models that don't expose logprobs.
  • Citation verification — if the model is asked to cite sources, programmatically check that the cited source actually contains the claimed fact, rather than trusting the citation exists just because it's present.

§ 10.6 — HALLUCINATION MITIGATION
Thought experiment

If you could only make one change to reduce hallucination in a production system — improving the retrieval pipeline, or fine-tuning the model to be more cautious — which would you expect to move the needle further, and why might the honest answer be "it depends on where the hallucinations are actually coming from"?

Reducing fabrication at the source

MitigationMechanism
Retrieval grounding (RAG)Give the model relevant, correct source material at generation time rather than relying on parametric memory — see Study Note 014
Explicit "don't know" instructionPrompting the model to explicitly decline when context is insufficient, rather than defaulting to a confident guess
Citation requirementsForcing the model to attribute every claim to a source makes ungrounded fabrication more visible and programmatically checkable
Decoding strategyLower temperature reduces variance/creativity, which can reduce fabrication risk for factual tasks (at the cost of more repetitive output)
Fine-tuning for calibrationTraining the model on examples where it correctly abstains from answering, so it learns when to say "I don't know" instead of always attempting an answer
Constrained/structured generationFor extraction-style tasks, forcing output into a schema (JSON with required fields sourced from context) limits the surface area for free-form fabrication
Post-hoc verification passA second LLM call (or the detection techniques from §10.5) checks the first response's claims against source material before it's shown to the user
The honest framing Hallucination cannot be reduced to zero with current LLM architectures — the goal is to reduce its frequency, and more importantly, to build systems that catch it before the user sees it rather than pretending prevention alone is sufficient. Detection (§10.5) and mitigation are a layered defense, not a single fix.
§ 10.7 — GUARDRAILS
Thought experiment

A well-aligned model will usually refuse a directly harmful request. But what happens when a user doesn't ask directly — they wrap the request in a fictional story, a "hypothetical for research," or a multi-step conversation that slowly walks the model somewhere it wouldn't have gone in one step? Is refusing harmful requests actually the model's job alone?

Guardrails as a separate, explicit safety layer

Relying purely on a model's own trained-in judgment for safety is fragile — it's one layer, and it can be bypassed. Production systems add explicit, deterministic (or semi-deterministic) guardrails that sit outside the model's own reasoning, checking input before it reaches the model and output before it reaches the user.

   │  input   │   │ guardrails:    │   │         │   │ guardrails:     │   │  sees    │
   │          │   │ - PII detect   │   │         │   │ - fact/citation │   │  response│

   │          │   │ - topic scope  │   │         │   │   filter        │   │          │

Guardrails treat the LLM as an untrusted component sandwiched between two independent checkpoints — never assume the model's own alignment training is a sufficient safety boundary on its own.

Categories of guardrails

  • Input guardrails — PII/sensitive-data detection before it's sent to a third-party model API, prompt-injection pattern detection, topic/scope restriction (e.g. blocking off-topic or clearly adversarial queries), rate limiting.
  • Output guardrails — toxicity/content moderation, PII leakage checks (making sure the model didn't echo back sensitive data), schema/format validation for structured outputs, fact-checking against source (§10.5/10.6), and refusal-of-scope checks (e.g. blocking the model from giving specific investment advice in a regulated context).
  • Structural guardrails — treating retrieved/external content as untrusted data rather than instructions (see prompt-injection note in Study Note 014), sandboxing tool-execution permissions, and least-privilege scoping of what data/tools the model can actually touch.

Common tools/frameworks

Guardrails AISchema/output validation framework — enforces structured output, retries on validation failure

§ 10.8 — RED-TEAMING
Thought experiment

Your guardrails pass every test case your team wrote. Does that tell you the system is safe — or does it only tell you the system is safe against the failure modes your team happened to imagine? Who's playing the role of an actual adversary before your users' actual adversaries do?

Adversarial testing as a discipline, not a one-off exercise

Red-teaming is the deliberate, adversarial probing of a system to find failure modes before real attackers or real users find them by accident. It's fundamentally different from standard QA because the goal isn't "does the happy path work," it's "what's the worst input someone could deliberately construct."

Common attack categories to test for

  • Jailbreaking — prompts designed to bypass safety training (role-play framing, "DAN"-style persona injection, hypothetical/fictional wrapping, encoding tricks).
  • Prompt injection — malicious instructions embedded in retrieved documents, tool outputs, or user-uploaded files that try to override the system prompt's actual instructions.
  • Data extraction — attempts to extract system prompts, training data, or other users' data through carefully crafted queries.
  • Goal hijacking — in agentic/tool-using systems, manipulating the model into taking unintended actions (e.g. calling a tool it shouldn't, with parameters it shouldn't use).
  • Multi-turn erosion — attacks that build up gradually across a conversation rather than in a single message, exploiting the model's tendency to maintain conversational consistency once it's agreed to something.

Manual vs automated red-teaming

ApproachStrengthLimit
Manual (human red-teamers)Creative, finds genuinely novel attack patternsSlow, doesn't scale, expensive
Automated (adversarial LLM generates attacks)Scales to thousands of variations quickly, good for regression testing known attack classesLimited to patterns it's been designed/trained to try

Tools like Microsoft's PyRIT and NVIDIA's garak automate large-scale adversarial probing across known jailbreak/injection technique libraries, letting a team run red-team regression tests continuously (similar in spirit to running a security scanner in CI) rather than only before a big launch.

Why this can't be a one-time pre-launch exercise New jailbreak techniques are discovered continuously, and every prompt/model update can silently reopen a previously-closed vulnerability. Red-teaming needs to be a recurring practice tied to your deployment pipeline, not a checkbox exercise before the first launch.
§ 10.9 — ALIGNMENT BASICS
Thought experiment

A base language model, trained only to predict the next token from internet text, is not the same thing as a helpful, harmless assistant that follows instructions and declines harmful requests. What actually happens between "predicts plausible next words" and "behaves like ChatGPT" — and why can't you get there with pretraining alone?

How raw next-token prediction becomes an aligned assistant

A pretrained base model is a powerful but unruly text-completion engine — it will complete a harmful instruction just as readily as a benign one, because it has no built-in notion of "should." Alignment is the set of post-training techniques that shape this raw capability into behavior that follows instructions, avoids harm, and reflects human preferences.

Core techniques, at a conceptual level

  • Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) — the base model is fine-tuned on curated examples of instruction → good response pairs, teaching it the basic shape of "being helpful and following instructions" before any preference optimization begins.
  • RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) — human raters compare pairs of model outputs and indicate which is preferred; those preferences train a separate reward model, which then guides reinforcement learning (commonly PPO) to steer the LLM toward higher-reward (more preferred) outputs.
  • DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) — a more recent, simpler alternative to RLHF that directly optimizes the model on preference pairs without training a separate reward model or running full RL — mathematically shown to converge toward a similar objective with a simpler, more stable training pipeline.
  • RLAIF (RL from AI Feedback) — replaces (or supplements) human preference labels with AI-generated preference judgments, trading some quality for dramatically lower labeling cost and higher scale.
  • Constitutional AI — instead of relying solely on human-labeled preferences, the model is guided by an explicit written set of principles ("a constitution"); the model critiques and revises its own outputs against these principles, and this self-critique data is used for further training — reducing dependence on large volumes of human labels for every safety judgment.

   Pretrained          Supervised           Preference            Aligned
   base model    ───▶  fine-tuning   ───▶   optimization   ───▶   assistant
   (raw next-          (SFT: learn          (RLHF / DPO /         model
   token predictor)     "how to be          RLAIF: learn
                        helpful")            "what's actually
                                              preferred/safe")
Each stage narrows the space of behaviors: pretraining gives broad capability, SFT gives basic instruction-following shape, preference optimization sharpens toward human-preferred, safer behavior.
Why this matters even if you never train a model yourself Understanding alignment as a layered, imperfect process — not a switch that's flipped once — explains why guardrails, red-teaming, and evaluation (§10.1-10.8) remain necessary even on top of a well-aligned foundation model. Alignment training reduces but does not eliminate the need for application-level safety engineering.
PART 11 / 11

AI Infrastructure & Ops

The part of the system that decides whether your LLM app survives contact with real traffic, real cost constraints, and a real on-call rotation.

§ 11.1 — WHY LLM SERVING IS DIFFERENT
Thought experiment

You've spent sixteen years scaling stateless REST APIs and distributed microservices, where a request either completes fast or times out and gets retried. An LLM inference request can take anywhere from 200ms to 30+ seconds depending on output length, and holds GPU memory hostage for its entire duration. Does everything you know about horizontal scaling and load balancing still apply directly — or does something fundamental change?

The core difference: autoregressive, stateful, memory-bound

Serving an LLM is architecturally closer to serving a long-running stateful stream than a stateless request/response API, for three reasons that don't exist in typical microservice serving:

  • Autoregressive generation — the model produces one token at a time, each depending on all previous tokens; a response isn't computed in one shot, it's a sequential loop, so latency scales with output length in a way a typical API call doesn't.
  • The KV cache — to avoid recomputing attention over the entire sequence at every new token, the model caches key/value tensors for all previous tokens in GPU memory for the duration of that request. This cache grows with sequence length and directly competes with other concurrent requests for the same limited GPU memory — it's the central resource-management problem in LLM serving.
  • Batching is fundamentally different — requests in a batch don't finish at the same time (different output lengths), so naive static batching wastes GPU cycles waiting for the slowest request in the batch to finish before starting the next batch.
The one-line reframe Traditional API scaling is about handling more concurrent requests. LLM serving is about efficiently managing a scarce, shared resource — GPU memory occupied by KV caches — across requests that arrive and finish at unpredictable, staggered times. This is why purpose-built inference servers exist instead of just wrapping a model in a Flask app behind a load balancer.
§ 11.2 — SERVING ENGINES
CORE INFRA
Thought experiment

If naive batching wastes GPU cycles waiting for the slowest request, and naive memory allocation wastes GPU memory reserving worst-case space for every request's KV cache — what would an inference server look like if it were purpose-built to solve exactly those two problems?

vLLM, TGI, and Triton — solving the batching/memory problem

vLLM — PagedAttention and continuous batching

vLLM's headline innovation is PagedAttention, which borrows the idea of virtual memory paging from operating systems: instead of allocating one large, contiguous block of GPU memory per request's KV cache (which forces over-allocation for worst-case sequence length and causes fragmentation), it manages the KV cache in small, fixed-size "pages" that can be allocated non-contiguously and shared across requests where possible (e.g. shared prefixes). Combined with continuous batching — where finished requests are immediately replaced by new ones in the batch rather than waiting for the whole batch to complete — this dramatically improves GPU utilization and throughput.

TGI (Text Generation Inference) — Hugging Face's serving stack

TGI is Hugging Face's production inference server, also implementing continuous batching and optimized attention kernels, tightly integrated with the Hugging Face model ecosystem, with strong support for quantization and tensor parallelism out of the box. It's often the path of least friction when your models already live in the HF ecosystem.

Triton Inference Server — the general-purpose, multi-framework option

NVIDIA Triton is a broader model-serving platform (not LLM-specific) supporting multiple frameworks (PyTorch, TensorFlow, ONNX, TensorRT) and model types side-by-side, with strong support for dynamic batching, model ensembles, and multi-model deployment on shared infrastructure. For LLMs specifically, it's commonly paired with TensorRT-LLM as its backend for optimized inference kernels.

Grounding this in a platform-engineering lens If you've tuned thread pools and connection pools for high-throughput microservices, the mental model transfers directly: continuous batching is conceptually similar to not letting one slow downstream call block an entire worker pool — the serving engine is doing dynamic, fine-grained resource scheduling instead of coarse, batch-level scheduling.
§ 11.3 — COST & LATENCY
Thought experiment

Two levers exist to make an LLM system cheaper and faster: shrink the model, or be smarter about which requests even need the biggest model in the first place. Which lever do most teams reach for first — and which one usually has the higher ceiling?

Where the money and milliseconds actually go

Quantization — shrinking the model itself

Model weights are typically trained in 16-bit or 32-bit floating point. Quantization reduces the numeric precision of weights (and sometimes activations) to 8-bit, 4-bit, or lower, cutting memory footprint and often improving inference speed, at some cost to output quality (usually small if done carefully).

MethodNotes
GPTQPost-training quantization using layer-wise error compensation; strong quality retention at 4-bit
AWQ (Activation-aware Weight Quantization)Protects the small subset of weights most important to activations, often outperforming GPTQ at similar bit-widths
GGUFQuantization format popularized by llama.cpp, widely used for CPU/edge and consumer-GPU inference
bitsandbytes (8-bit/4-bit)Common in-training and inference quantization library integrated with Hugging Face tooling

Speculative decoding

A small, fast "draft" model proposes several tokens ahead; the larger target model verifies them in a single forward pass instead of generating token-by-token itself. When the draft model's guesses are accepted, generation effectively speeds up several-fold, since the expensive large model is doing verification (parallelizable) rather than sequential generation for every single token.

Model routing / cascading

Not every query needs your most expensive, most capable model. Routing sends simple/cheap queries to a smaller, faster, cheaper model and only escalates to a larger model when needed (based on query complexity classification, or a "try cheap first, escalate on low-confidence" cascade). This is often the single highest-leverage cost optimization available, because it directly reduces how often you pay for the expensive model at all — no serving infrastructure change required.

Batching, streaming, and output-length control

  • Request batching — covered in §11.2, the serving-engine-level fix.
  • Streaming responses — doesn't reduce total cost, but dramatically improves perceived latency by showing tokens as they're generated rather than waiting for the full response.
  • Constraining max output length / stop sequences — a simple, often-overlooked lever: unconstrained generation can run far longer (and cost far more) than the task actually needs.

In most real traffic distributions, a large share of queries are simple enough for a cheaper model — routing captures that savings without touching model quality on the hard cases.
§ 11.4 — CACHING
Thought experiment

Classical caching keys on exact input match — same URL, same cache key, same response. If two users ask "what's the exit load for early redemption" and "what penalty applies if I redeem early," these are different strings but effectively the same question. Can a cache built for exact-match ever help here — and if not, what has to change about caching itself?

Caching strategies unique to LLM systems

Exact-match prompt caching

The simplest layer — if the exact same prompt (or exact same prefix) has been seen before, reuse the cached response, or reuse cached KV-cache state for a shared prefix (e.g. a long, repeated system prompt) so the model doesn't recompute attention over it for every request. Several LLM APIs now expose native prompt/prefix caching, discounting or speeding up requests that reuse a previously-seen prefix.

Semantic caching

Instead of matching on exact string equality, embed the incoming query (the same embedding technique from RAG, Study Note 014) and check for a cached response to a semantically similar prior query above a similarity threshold. This catches paraphrased repeats of common questions — high-value in customer-support/FAQ-style traffic where the same underlying question arrives phrased a hundred different ways.

KV-cache reuse across requests

Distinct from prompt caching at the API level — this is a serving-engine-level optimization (vLLM and others support this) where the computed key/value tensors for a shared prefix (e.g. the same system prompt or the same retrieved document across multiple users' queries) are reused across separate inference requests rather than recomputed, saving both compute and time-to-first-token.

Caching layerCatchesRisk
Exact-matchLiteral repeat requestsVery high precision, but low hit rate for varied user phrasing

Where semantic caching bites you "What's the interest rate for a 1-year loan?" and "what's the interest rate for a 5-year loan?" can be embedded as highly similar — a semantic cache with too loose a threshold will confidently return the wrong number. Semantic caching works best on FAQ-style, parameter-free questions, and needs careful threshold tuning (and ideally a numeric/entity-aware guard) before being trusted on anything with variable specifics.
§ 11.5 — OBSERVABILITY
CORE INFRA
Thought experiment

In a traditional distributed system, you'd never ship without structured logs, distributed traces, and dashboards — you'd know exactly which service, which call, which line failed. Your RAG pipeline just gave a wrong answer to a real user. Which of the five stages — query rewrite, retrieval, reranking, prompt assembly, generation — actually failed? Without the LLM equivalent of tracing, how would you even begin to find out?

Why LLM apps need their own observability stack

Standard APM tools (think Datadog, New Relic) are built around latency, error rates, and structured logs for deterministic code paths. LLM applications need a parallel layer of observability purpose-built for tracing multi-step chains, capturing prompts/completions, and evaluating quality — not just uptime.

What LLM observability specifically needs to capture

  • Full trace of a multi-step pipeline — for a RAG or agentic system, every stage (query rewrite → retrieval → rerank → prompt assembly → generation → post-processing) needs to be individually visible, with inputs/outputs at each hop, so a bad final answer can be traced back to exactly where it went wrong (the same failure-attribution matrix from Study Note 014, §11).
  • Prompt/completion logging — the actual text sent to and received from the model, versioned alongside which prompt template/version produced it.
  • Token usage & cost tracking — per-request and aggregated token counts and cost, broken down by model, endpoint, and even by user/tenant for chargeback or budget alerting.
  • Latency breakdown — not just total latency, but time-to-first-token vs total generation time vs retrieval time vs reranking time, since these have very different optimization levers.
  • Online evaluation scores — running the LLM-as-judge/faithfulness checks from §10.3/§10.5 continuously on live (or sampled live) traffic, not just offline test sets, and surfacing quality regressions as a monitored metric like any other SLO.
  • User feedback signals — thumbs up/down, regenerate clicks, edit/correction actions — tied back to the specific trace that produced that response.

§ 11.6 — LLMOPS / MLOPS
Thought experiment

You've run CI/CD pipelines for deterministic code for years — a test suite either passes or fails, a deployment either works or gets rolled back. A prompt change makes 85% of your eval set better and 15% worse. Does that pass your pipeline? What does "passing" even mean when there's no single ground truth and no guaranteed reproducibility?

CI/CD for systems that don't have a single right answer

LLMOps extends classical MLOps practices (versioning, CI/CD, monitoring, reproducibility) to account for the specific properties of LLM-based systems: non-determinism, prompts as a first-class versioned artifact, and evaluation that's probabilistic rather than pass/fail.

What gets versioned (beyond just model weights)

  • Prompts/prompt templates — treated as code: version-controlled, code-reviewed, and tested, since a prompt change is a behavior change just as much as a code change.
  • Retrieval index/corpus version — for RAG systems, which document set and embedding model produced the current index, since retrieval quality is tied to both.
  • Model/endpoint version — which exact model version (including provider-side silent updates, a real risk with hosted APIs) served a given response.
  • Eval dataset version — the test set itself evolves as new failure modes are discovered, and needs versioning like any other test suite.

Deployment strategies adapted for LLM changes

StrategyHow it applies to LLM systems
Shadow deploymentRun the new prompt/model in parallel on live traffic without serving its output to users, comparing its outputs/eval scores against the current production version before cutover
Canary releaseRoute a small percentage of real traffic to the new version, monitor quality/cost/latency metrics, then ramp up gradually — critical because offline eval sets never perfectly represent live traffic distribution
A/B testing with online evalSplit traffic between prompt/model variants and compare not just business metrics (conversion, satisfaction) but the automated quality scores from §10.3/§11.5
RollbackBecause prompts/configs are versioned artifacts, rollback should be as fast as reverting a config — not a redeploy — since a bad prompt change can degrade quality just as sharply as bad code

§ 11.7 — ACTIVE RECALL

Test yourself — click each question to reveal the answer

Cover the answers first. Try to answer from memory before expanding.

Why is pairwise LLM-judge comparison generally more reliable than asking a judge to output an absolute 1-5 score?
Relative judgments ("which of these two is better") are easier for LLMs to make consistently than absolute scoring, which is more prone to scale drift and inconsistency across different calls. Pairwise comparison is also less sensitive to verbosity/style biases when both answers are shown together.
What's the difference between intrinsic and extrinsic hallucination, and why does the distinction matter for detection?
Intrinsic hallucination contradicts the given source/context (a faithfulness failure, detectable via NLI/entailment checking against the retrieved context). Extrinsic hallucination states something unverifiable against the source — not necessarily false, just unsupported. Different detection techniques target each: entailment checking for intrinsic, self-consistency sampling for both, but especially useful for catching fabricated specifics with no source to check against at all.
Why should guardrails never be treated as redundant with a model's own alignment training?
Alignment training is one layer and can be bypassed (jailbreaks, prompt injection, multi-turn erosion). Guardrails are an explicit, separate checkpoint outside the model's own reasoning — checking input before the model sees it and output before the user sees it — providing defense in depth rather than relying on a single point of safety.
What problem does PagedAttention (vLLM) actually solve?
It solves inefficient GPU memory management for the KV cache — instead of allocating one large contiguous memory block per request (causing over-allocation and fragmentation), it manages KV cache in small, non-contiguous pages that can be allocated flexibly and shared across requests where possible, similar to virtual memory paging in operating systems.
Why is model routing/cascading often the highest-leverage cost optimization, ahead of quantization?
Quantization reduces the cost of every request against a given model. Routing reduces how often you pay for the expensive model at all, by sending simple queries to a cheaper model and only escalating complex ones — since most real traffic distributions skew toward simple queries, this often yields larger aggregate savings without touching serving infrastructure at all.
Why is semantic caching riskier than exact-match caching, and where does it fail specifically?
Semantic caching matches on embedding similarity rather than exact text, so it can conflate questions that are conceptually close but require different specific answers — e.g. two questions differing only in a numeric parameter like loan tenure. It works best for FAQ-style, parameter-free questions and needs careful threshold tuning plus entity-aware guards for anything with variable specifics.
In LLMOps, why does "prompts as versioned artifacts" matter as much as versioning the model itself?
A prompt change is a behavior change just as significant as a code change — it directly affects output quality, safety, and cost. Without versioning, code review, and rollback capability for prompts, teams lose the ability to trace why quality shifted or to quickly revert a regression, since the model itself might be unchanged while the prompt is what actually broke.
Why does canary deployment matter more for LLM systems than a simple "test passed, ship it" pipeline?
Offline eval sets never perfectly represent the full distribution of live traffic — a prompt/model change can look like an improvement on your test set while quietly regressing on edge cases your eval set doesn't cover. Canary release exposes the change to a small slice of real traffic first, letting you catch distributional surprises before a full rollout.
