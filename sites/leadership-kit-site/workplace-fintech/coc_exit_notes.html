1. Financed amount — how the ₹19.2L splits

The residual value (₹1,00,000) is typically carved out separately — it's what you pay at the end to complete the ownership transfer. That leaves the amount actually financed via EMI:

₹19,20,000 − ₹1,00,000 = ₹18,20,000 financed over 60 months.

Working backward from your EMI of ₹40,000/month for 60 months on a principal of ₹18,20,000, the implied interest rate is approximately 11.5% per annum (reducing balance basis).

This is a back-calculated estimate. Your actual sanction letter/loan schedule will state the exact rate — worth confirming with HR/Finance since a slightly different rate changes your outstanding balance below.
2. How much do I still owe right now (2.5 years in)?

This is the number that matters most for your exit — not "remaining EMIs × 40,000" (that double-counts future interest), but the outstanding principal on a reducing-balance schedule.

Why is it still ~₹10.4L when you've paid ₹12L on an ₹18.2L loan? Because reducing-balance EMIs are interest-heavy in the early years — a larger share of your first 30 EMIs went toward interest, not principal. This is standard and not specific to your bank.

Ask Finance for the actual amortization schedule / current outstanding principal figure — this is your single most important number before resigning.
3. What are my two realistic exit options?

Option A — Settle the loan and keep the car. You pay the outstanding principal (≈₹10.4L) plus any foreclosure/processing charges the policy specifies (commonly 1–3% of outstanding, but check your document — it may be nil). Ownership transfers once cleared, likely without needing to separately pay the ₹1L residual since that gets folded into the foreclosure settlement.

Option B — Return the car. Company takes the car back and sells it or revalues it internally. If the value recovered is less than your outstanding ₹10.4L, you pay the shortfall. If more, some companies refund the difference — many don't. Check your specific clause.

You cannot simply "keep paying EMIs" after resigning — salary deduction and the company's loan guarantee end with employment, so the loan gets closed out one way or the other at exit.

Exiting early and settling now works out cheaper in absolute terms, since you skip the remaining interest — but you're paying a large lump sum upfront instead of spreading it.

5. What exactly should I ask HR/Finance before deciding?
  • What is the exact outstanding principal on my loan today?
  • Is there a foreclosure or early-settlement charge, and how much?
  • If I settle and keep the car, does the ₹1L residual still apply separately, or is it absorbed into the settlement?
  • If I return the car instead, how is the vehicle valued, and am I refunded any surplus?
  • What is the deadline for settlement relative to my last working day?
  • What documents will I get (NOC, RC transfer, loan closure letter) once settled?
6. Bottom line for your situation

At 2.5 years, you've paid ₹12,00,000 but still owe roughly ₹10,39,000 in principal. Settling this (plus any foreclosure charge) to keep the car will likely cost somewhere around ₹10.5L, versus returning the car and having the shortfall (if any) adjusted from its resale/revaluation.

This estimate uses a back-calculated ~11.5% p.a. reducing-balance rate. The one document that will make this exact rather than estimated is your loan amortization schedule from Finance — ask for it before you resign.
