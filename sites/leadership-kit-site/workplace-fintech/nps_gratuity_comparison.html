Retirement Benefits

NPS, Gratuity & How They Compare

One is funded entirely by your employer and can't be touched before exit. The other lives in a market account and compounds regardless of where you work. They solve the same retirement problem — very differently.

Part 1 — NPS Basics
1. What is NPS (National Pension System)?

A voluntary, market-linked retirement savings scheme regulated by PFRDA. Unlike EPF (fixed government-declared interest) or Gratuity (employer-funded lump sum), NPS returns depend on how your money is invested across equity, corporate bonds, and government securities — you (or a default option) choose the asset mix.

2. Tier 1 vs Tier 2 — what's the difference?

When people say "NPS" in a salary/tax context, they almost always mean Tier 1.

80CCD(2) — the employer's NPS contribution — is one of the very few deductions that survives under the New Tax Regime. This is why it's often highlighted as a smart FBP component regardless of which regime you're on.
4. What happens to my NPS corpus at retirement (age 60)? 
At age 60, NPS corpus splits:

Up to 60% → Lump sum withdrawal
             (tax-free)

At least 40% → Mandatory annuity purchase
               (from an insurer; monthly pension
                payouts are taxable as income
                when received)

So NPS is not a fully tax-free retirement payout like Gratuity or EPF — the mandatory annuity portion converts into a taxable income stream for the rest of your life.

Part 2 — Gratuity Basics
5. What is Gratuity, and how is it different from EPF/NPS?

Gratuity is a lump-sum benefit paid entirely by your employer — you don't contribute anything from your salary toward it (unlike EPF and NPS, which are salary deductions). It's a statutory obligation under the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972 (now part of the Code on Social Security, 2020), for any establishment with 10+ employees.

6. When am I eligible, and how is it calculated?

Eligibility: 5 years of continuous service (waived for death or disablement). A widely recognized exception: completing 4 years + 240 working days in the 5th year is often accepted as meeting the requirement.

Formula (for employers covered under the Act):

Gratuity = (Last drawn Basic + DA) × 15 × (years of service) / 26

The "15/26" reflects 15 days' wages per year of service, using 26 as the standard working days in a month under the Act.

7. How is Gratuity taxed?
CategoryExemption
Government employeesFully exempt — no cap
Private sector (covered under the Act)Least of: ₹20 lakh, the 15/26 formula amount, or actual gratuity received
Private sector (not covered under the Act)Least of: ₹20 lakh, half-month average salary × years, or actual gratuity received

The ₹20 lakh exemption is a lifetime cap across all employers, not a per-employer limit — if you've already claimed exemption on gratuity from a previous job, it reduces what you can claim on a future payout.

Under the New Tax Regime, a CBDT circular clarified that gratuity is exempt only up to ₹5 lakh (not the full ₹20 lakh available under the Old Regime) — worth factoring into your regime choice in your final year with an employer, or at retirement.

9. Which one should I actually think about more actively?

Gratuity is largely passive from your side — there's no "contribution decision" to make, it just accrues based on tenure and last-drawn salary. The main thing worth tracking is your years of continuous service as you approach the 5-year mark, especially before a resignation.

NPS is the one with actual decisions to make: how much to voluntarily contribute (beyond any employer match), which asset allocation (equity/debt mix) to choose, and whether to route employer benefits through 80CCD(2) as part of your Flexible Benefits — since that piece works in both tax regimes.
