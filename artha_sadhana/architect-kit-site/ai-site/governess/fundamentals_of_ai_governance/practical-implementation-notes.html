Writing AI Policies, Requirements & Internal Guidelines

Implementation · Documentation Tiers
›
RecallHow can an organization create AI governance documents that are actually used by teams, rather than becoming shelf-ware, and what is the difference between a policy, a requirement, and a guideline?
Use a three-tier model: a short, C-suite-approved Policy that sets principles and boundaries; mandatory, auditable Requirements ("shall" statements) enforced through existing workflows; and flexible, educational Guidelines (templates, FAQs, case studies) that help teams execute them. Start minimal and expand only as the AI portfolio's maturity demands it.

The Three-Tier Documentation Model

AI governance documentation must be practical, accessible, and role-appropriate. It's not about producing a thick manual nobody reads. There are three distinct levels:

1. AI Policy (Strategic Framework)

A short, board-approved document (2–4 pages) that defines the organization's stance on responsible AI. It answers:

  • What do we mean by "responsible AI" in our context?
  • What core principles (fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy) apply to all AI projects?
  • What types of AI use are permitted, restricted (e.g. emotion recognition), or prohibited (e.g. social scoring)?
  • Who has authority to approve AI projects, and at what risk level?
  • How does the AI policy align with existing ethics, data protection, cybersecurity, and sustainability policies?

The policy is the "constitution" — it sets boundaries and delegates authority. It must be signed by the C-suite to carry weight.

2. Corporate Requirements (Operational Standards)

More specific, mandatory rules that every AI project must follow. Examples:

  • Every AI system must have a named responsible owner (not a team)
  • Training data must be evaluated for bias, provenance, and consent before use
  • No model may be deployed without a cross-functional review (technical, legal, business as minimum)
  • Key decisions must be logged and traceable across the lifecycle
  • A DPIA must be completed for any system processing personal data

These are often expressed as a checklist or a set of "shall" statements. They are auditable and non-negotiable.

3. Internal Guidelines (Best Practice & How-To)

Flexible, educational documents that help teams execute the requirements. They might contain:

  • Templates and worked examples (e.g. a filled-out data justification report)
  • Self-assessment questionnaires (ethical, legal, risk)
  • Case studies of successful and failed projects from within the organization
  • Guidance tailored to different audiences (data scientists vs. business analysts vs. procurement)

Guidelines should be concise, searchable, and iteratively improved based on user feedback.

Implementation Tip: Start Minimal, Evolve

  • Begin with a simple AI policy, a one-page requirements checklist, and a short guidance note
  • Enforce the requirements through existing workflows (e.g. project intake, architecture review)
  • Expand documentation only when the organisation's AI portfolio and maturity demand it

Version Control, Decision Logs & Traceability

Implementation · Traceability
›
RecallWhy are version control and a justified decision log not just good engineering practice, but legal and operational imperatives for AI governance?
Without them, an organization loses track of which model version is live, what data trained it, and why choices were made — a blind spot that undermines audits, incident response, and defensibility. A model version register plus a justified decision log (decision, decision-maker, alternatives considered, rationale) together form the contemporaneous record of due diligence regulators and courts expect.

The Problem of Drift and Lost Rationale

AI models evolve — retraining, fine-tuning, data changes. Without version control, an organization quickly loses track of which model version is in production, what data it was trained on, and why certain choices were made. This is a governance blind spot that undermines audits, incident response, and continuous improvement.

Model Version Register

Every AI system should maintain a single source of truth for its lineage:

  • Unique model identifier and current version number
  • Date of creation/modification
  • Changes introduced (new data, hyperparameters, feature engineering, environment)
  • Who approved the change and based on what evaluation
  • Validation test results for the new version (performance, fairness, robustness)
  • Link to the data version and code commit

This can be managed in a model registry (MLflow, Kubeflow) or a well-structured documented repository. The essential thing is accessibility and discipline.

Justified Decision Log

At every critical lifecycle gate (design, data selection, model choice, deployment, retirement), significant decisions must be logged with:

  • Decision: what was chosen or decided
  • Decision-maker: who (name, role)
  • Date & context: when and under what circumstances
  • Alternatives considered: what other options were evaluated
  • Risk assessment: what risks were identified and how they were mitigated
  • Rationale: why this path was chosen over alternatives

Examples: "Selected dataset X over Y because Y lacked consent for commercial use." "Excluded variable Z as it was a proxy for race, despite improving accuracy by 2%."

Governance Value

  • Audits: regulators or internal audit can trace every critical choice
  • Defensibility: if a model is challenged, you have a contemporaneous record of due diligence
  • Knowledge continuity: team changes don't result in lost understanding
  • Continuous improvement: patterns in past decisions inform future projects

Agile Governance Tools: Templates, Minimal Records & Checklists

Implementation · Agile Tooling
›
RecallHow can an organization keep AI governance lightweight and efficient while still meeting regulatory standards, using three simple tools?
Standardize the repetitive parts so humans focus on judgment calls: Templates (project sheet, impact assessment, validation report), the Minimal Record / Model Passport (a living one-screen fact sheet per model), and a gate-based Operational Checklist that doubles as formal evidence governance steps were completed.

Governance Doesn't Have to Be Bureaucratic

The secret is to standardize the repetitive parts so humans can focus on the judgment calls. Three tools form an agile governance backbone:

1. Templates (Pre-structured Documents)
  • AI Project Sheet: use case, objective, data sources, responsible parties, expected risks, success criteria (1–2 pages)
  • Impact Assessment Template: prompts for ethical, legal, privacy, and fundamental rights impacts, tailored to risk level
  • Validation Report Template: standard format for performance metrics, fairness results, robustness tests, and reviewer comments

Templates ensure nothing is forgotten and allow comparability across projects.

2. Minimal Records (The Model Passport)

Every AI system must have a living, minimal record containing:

  • Unique ID and current version
  • Purpose and limitations (plain language)
  • List of variables used and their sources
  • Deployment date and environment
  • Functional owner (business) and technical owner (engineering)
  • Change history (links to version register)

This can live in a spreadsheet, a data catalog, or a dedicated governance platform. The key: always updated, easily accessible.

3. Operational Checklist (Gate-Based)

A simple checklist that follows the lifecycle. Sample items:

  • Data quality validated?
  • Bias risk assessed and documented?
  • Model selection justified and explainability considered?
  • Cross-functional review completed (legal, tech, business)?
  • Post-launch monitoring plan defined?
  • Fallback and rollback procedures documented?

Checklists serve as both a memory aid and formal evidence that governance steps were completed. They can be integrated into project management tools (Jira, Asana) or CI/CD pipelines.

The Agile Mindset

These three tools make governance replicable, scalable, and self-service. Teams don't need a governance expert standing over them; they follow the templates and checklists, and the minimal record ensures visibility.

Standardizing Templates: The Key to Scaling Governance

Implementation · Scaling
›
RecallHow can an organization avoid becoming dependent on a handful of governance experts, and why do standardized templates hold the answer?
Relying on one or two experts to review every project is a single point of failure that doesn't scale. Standardized templates encode the organization's governance know-how so any team can start a project asking the right questions without waiting for an expert — trading tacit expert knowledge for a reusable, version-controlled library.

The Expert Bottleneck

Many organizations rely on one or two people (a chief data scientist, a privacy lawyer) to review every AI project. This doesn't scale, creates delays, and becomes a single point of failure. Standardized templates democratize governance knowledge.

Designing Reusable Templates

  • Standard Project Form: mandatory fields for purpose, data, stakeholders, risk self-assessment
  • Data Description Form: origin, license, consent status, variables, known biases — forces critical thinking about data before modelling
  • Ethical Impact Quick Screen: a short questionnaire (5–7 questions) that flags if a full impact assessment is needed
  • Pre-Deployment Review Checklist: aggregates evidence from previous phases

Benefits

  • Autonomy: any team can start a project with the right questions without waiting for an expert
  • Consistency: all projects are documented the same way, making portfolio management and auditing easier
  • Embedded Learning: templates guide users to think about governance early; they are educational tools, not just forms
  • Scalability: the organization can increase AI projects linearly without increasing governance headcount

Implementation Advice

  • Co-design templates with the people who will use them. Usability drives adoption.
  • Keep them short: a template that takes more than 30 minutes to fill out is too long.
  • Version-control the templates themselves; improve them based on user feedback.
  • Pair templates with short training videos or guides.

Case Study: AI Credit Scoring in Financial Services

Case Study · Finance
›
RecallWhat specific governance challenges arise when a bank deploys an ML-based credit scoring model, and how should they be addressed to ensure fairness, explainability, and regulatory compliance?
Five challenges: explainability (right to a meaningful reason for denial), indirect discrimination via proxies, data protection/DPIA, full audit-trail traceability, and mandatory human oversight for denials. Addressed through pre-deployment bias testing and committee sign-off, live monitoring by subgroup, and clear customer communication with an appeal channel.

The Use Case

A bank deploys a machine learning model to assess loan applicants' creditworthiness. The model uses credit history, transactional data, digital behaviour, and self-declared variables. It outperforms traditional scorecards in predictive power and enables faster digital approvals.

Governance Challenges

1. Explainability & Right to Explanation

If a loan is denied, the applicant has the right (under GDPR Art. 22 and many national laws) to know why. The bank must provide a meaningful explanation in plain language — e.g. "Your application was declined due to a high debt-to-income ratio and recent missed payments." Black-box models without interpretability tools are a legal liability.

2. Algorithmic Discrimination

Even if gender, race, or age are excluded, proxies (e.g. postal code, type of employer) can cause indirect discrimination. The model must be tested with fairness metrics (equal opportunity, demographic parity difference) across protected groups. Subgroup analysis is mandatory.

3. Data Protection & Consent

Credit scoring uses personal data. The bank needs a lawful basis (likely legitimate interest or contractual necessity) and must transparently inform applicants. A DPIA is required. The DPO must be involved early.

4. Traceability & Audit Trail

Every credit decision must be logged: input data, model version, score, reason codes, and whether a human override occurred. This is critical for handling disputes, regulatory audits, and internal model risk management.

5. Human Oversight

Fully automated denial without human review is restricted. The bank must provide a channel for applicants to contest the decision and have a human review it.

Governance Framework in Practice

  • Pre-Deployment: bias testing, fairness thresholds, explainability validation, DPIA, and approval by a dedicated AI/Model Risk Committee
  • Production Monitoring: track score distributions, approval rates by subgroup, drift detection — set alerts for anomalous shifts
  • Customer Communication: provide a clear notice of automated processing, a plain-language explanation, and instructions for appeal

Case Study: AI Medical Diagnosis in Healthcare

Case Study · Healthcare
›
RecallHow does AI governance adapt when a model assists in diagnosing life-threatening conditions from medical images, and what unique requirements arise around clinical safety and human responsibility?
Governance shifts to a safety-critical mindset: explanations must be clinically meaningful (not just a heatmap), validation must use representative local patient data with mandatory human review of every diagnosis, bias audits must cover subgroup performance, the system is regulated as a medical device (SaMD), and clear liability lines between clinician, hospital, and vendor must be pre-established.

The Use Case

A hospital uses an AI system to detect potential tumors in chest X-rays or mammograms. The model demonstrates high accuracy, sometimes exceeding radiologists, but its outputs influence treatment decisions.

Governance Challenges

1. Clinical Explainability

A radiologist must understand why the model flagged a region as suspicious. It's not enough to show a heatmap; the explanation must be clinically meaningful (e.g. "mass with spiculated margins, density X, correlated with feature Y"). Without this, the clinician cannot take responsibility.

2. Patient Safety & Robustness

A false negative can be fatal. The model must be validated on representative, local patient data, not just a public benchmark. Performance must be monitored continuously. There must be mandatory human review of every diagnosis — AI is a support tool, not the final decision-maker.

3. Bias & Representativeness

If the model was trained on a population (e.g. predominantly light-skinned, Western) different from the served population, it may perform poorly for underrepresented groups. Bias audits and performance by subgroup (age, sex, ethnicity) are essential.

4. Regulatory Classification

In the EU, diagnostic AI is a medical device (Software as a Medical Device, SaMD). It requires conformity assessment, CE marking, and post-market surveillance. The governance must align with MDR (Medical Device Regulation) and IVDR.

5. Ethical & Informed Consent

Patients should be informed that AI is used in their diagnosis and have the right to refuse. Who is liable if the AI misses a tumor that a human would have caught? Clear lines of accountability between clinician, hospital, and AI vendor must be established.

Governance Approach

  • Clinical Validation Committee (radiologists, data scientists, risk managers, legal, patient representative)
  • Locked model versions with rigorous regression testing before updates
  • Full audit trail of every AI-assisted diagnosis: image, model version, output, clinician's interpretation, final decision
  • Continuous monitoring for drift and subgroup performance

Case Study: Generative AI — Content Risks & Unpredictability

Case Study · Generative AI
›
RecallWhy does generative AI break traditional governance models, and what new controls are needed for systems that produce novel, unpredictable content?
Unlike a classifier, output cannot be pre-approved — a generative model can produce anything within its learned distribution, including offensive, hallucinated, or IP-infringing content. Governance shifts to post-hoc controls: automated content filters, a human review queue for sensitive categories, usage guardrails, prompt/output logging, vendor due diligence on training data provenance, and preference for curated/fine-tuned models over general-purpose ones.

The Use Case

An organization deploys a generative AI tool: a customer service chatbot, a marketing content generator, or an internal code assistant. These models produce text, images, or code on demand based on prompts.

Unique Governance Challenges

1. Unpredictable Outputs

Unlike a classifier, a generative model can produce anything within its learned distribution — including offensive, biased, hallucinated, or legally problematic content. The output cannot be pre-approved. Governance must therefore include post-hoc review and filtering.

2. Reputational & Legal Risk
  • A chatbot gives incorrect financial advice → customer harm, regulatory action
  • A marketing generator creates culturally insensitive imagery → brand crisis
  • A code assistant reproduces GPL-licensed code → IP infringement
3. Copyright & Intellectual Property

Many generative models are trained on publicly available but copyrighted data. Using outputs commercially without understanding the model's training data provenance exposes the organisation to IP lawsuits.

4. Transparency & User Confusion

Users may not know they are interacting with an AI. Deepfakes or synthetic media can be misleading. Regulations increasingly require labelling AI-generated content.

5. Accountability

Who is responsible for a harmful output — the prompt author, the model provider, or the deploying organization? Governance must define clear accountability, including human review for high-sensitivity outputs.

Governance Mitigations

  • Content Moderation Pipelines: automated filters (toxicity, factual accuracy) plus human review for high-risk outputs
  • Usage Policies & Guardrails: restrict prompts that request medical, legal, or financial advice; block generation of certain categories
  • Attribution & Logging: log all prompts and outputs for audit; label AI-generated content publicly
  • Vendor Due Diligence: when using third-party APIs, ensure contractual commitments on training data provenance, output filtering, and liability
  • Custom/Curated Models: prefer fine-tuned models on proprietary or verified data to reduce unpredictability

Case Study: AI in the Public Sector — Automated Decisions Affecting Citizens

Case Study · Public Sector
›
RecallWhat heightened governance obligations apply when a public authority uses AI to make or support decisions about citizens' rights, benefits, or access to services?
Public authority raises the bar: radical transparency (citizens must know AI was involved and how to appeal), plain-language explainability with human-override contestability, full audit-ready traceability, a mandatory pre-deployment Algorithmic Impact Assessment, and increasingly a public register of government algorithms with civil-society participation.

The Use Case

Public administrations deploy AI for: prioritizing social service cases, detecting benefits fraud, providing administrative information via chatbots, or automating inspections. These decisions directly affect individuals' livelihoods.

Heightened Governance Obligations

1. Radical Transparency

Citizens have a right to know:

  • That an automated system was involved in their case
  • What data was used, and for what purpose
  • The general logic of the decision
  • How to appeal and request a human review

This isn't just best practice — it's a legal obligation under the right to good administration and effective judicial protection.

2. Explainability & Contestability

Decisions must be explainable in non-technical language. There must be a simple, accessible appeal process with a human decision-maker who can override the algorithm.

3. Traceability & Audit-Ready Records

Public bodies must be able to demonstrate:

  • The model was trained on relevant, accurate, up-to-date data
  • Performance and fairness were validated before deployment
  • Monitoring is continuous and documented
  • A complete audit trail for each decision: input, model version, output, human override (if any)
4. Algorithmic Impact Assessment (AIA)

Before deployment, an AIA must be conducted to evaluate:

  • Risks to vulnerable groups (e.g. low-income, disabled, minorities)
  • Potential bias and its effect on equality
  • Impact on trust in public institutions

This is increasingly mandated by national laws.

5. Public Registers & Participation

Many jurisdictions are establishing public registers of government algorithms, listing purpose, logic, risk category, and oversight. Governance must be inclusive, involving legal experts, ethicists, DPOs, and civil society representatives.

Governance Framework

  • Algorithmic Transparency Officer or role within the existing data protection function
  • Mandatory AIA before any automated decision system is used
  • Citizen-facing explanation templates integrated into case management systems
  • Regular audits by an independent body or oversight committee
